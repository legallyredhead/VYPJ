Refusal to Listen: A Case Study of Digital Lynching and Mob-Mentality towards Phạm Tường Lan Thy in Vietnamese Internet Culture

Introduction

We tend to think that in this modern age, we have progressed immensely, our behaviors improved, and our thinking modern. Etiquettes are taught to our children at a young age. In public spaces we follow codes of conduct, and are most often told to “Treat others with respect”. However, in cyberspace, it seems as if all of our efforts for civil interactions are in vain - libel, slander, defamation are rife on all social media spaces. In all these dehumanizing acts a pattern of behavior emerged and has progressed into a ‘culture’ - digital lynching. One such case that epitomizes this toxic culture is that of Pham Tuong Lan Thy, a Vietnamese figure known for her achievements in the renowned academic TV show “Road to Olympia”. However, she has also become a victim of the digital mob mentality that has persisted for years. This op-eds uses the Hatred Framework to analyze Lan Thy’s story, underlining my perspective on this widely recognized case yet rarely discussed in Vietnam academia.

Case summary

For those unfamiliar with the case: At the end of 2015, Lan Thy faced widespread accusations claiming she had stolen a science experiment idea from a male friend, Gia Khoi, which allegedly led to his death from shock. On February 28, 2019, Lan Thy publicly refuted these claims, providing detailed evidence to defend herself. Despite this, she continued to face relentless harassment on Facebook, where users hurled insults and even encouraged her to harm herself.

Countless Facebook posts and groups concerning Lan Thy have been created for the past eight years, with most of them spreading shared hostility and branding her as the "Grey matter thief." Any netizens who attempt to question the accusations are quickly met with backlash. Rumors also circulated that her family was wealthy and influential enough to manipulate the media and her school's administration. As a result, Lan Thy has become a scapegoat for public frustration over corruption and the privileges of the wealthy .

Digital lynching in seven stages

The case's endurance can be summed up by three main causes: a) the sensational events and ambiguity of information, b) echo chambers and Vietnamese Facebook users' fixation on the past, and c) Lan Thy's background (and the lack of information thereof).

I’d also use the Hatred Framework as an analytical tool to dissect the triggers, escalation, and manifestations of hate towards Lan Thy. Developed by John Schafer and Joe Navarro, this framework offers a systematic approach to understanding the dynamics of hatred and hostility in cyberspace. This case contains all the steps outlined in the "7-Stage Hate Model".

In "Stage 1: The haters gather", Facebook groups with thousands of members were created to discuss and coordinate attacks against her, fueled by baseless accusations and "telephone game" rumors.

“Stage 2: The hate group defines itself" presents Lan Thy being labeled derogatory terms like "disabled sperm" and "freak," by these groups, and her achievements in academia disregarded and dismissed.

“Stage 3: The hate group disparates the target”, the hatred towards Lan Thy becomes "glue binds" for these online communities, with constant denigration and aggression aimed at her. Despite her attempts to defend herself with evidence, including specific details provided on February 28, 2019, Internet users refused to engage with her explanations, opting instead to continue emphasizing false narratives and escalating the hostility every year.

Haters then increase their taunts and offensive gestures towards Lan Thy to avoid introspection. This is “Stage 4: The hate group taunts the target.” Comments filled with insults and threats of self-harm or violence are prevalent across various online platforms.

"Stage 5: Hate group attacks the target without weapons" and "Stage 6: With weapons" While the attacks against Lan Thy remain primarily online, the intensity and aggressiveness of the language used by haters continue to rise. Calls for extreme measures such as "hiến tế nó" (sacrifice her) or "thiêu sống nó" (burn her alive) demonstrate the lengths to which some individuals are willing to go in their attempts to ruin her reputation and life.

Finally, “Stage 7: Hate group destroys the target”. By erasing her presence from the Internet and perpetuating false narratives about her, the group successfully silences Lan Thy's side of the story and dehumanizes her.

The analysis of Lan Thy's case highlights key insights into online behavior, misinformation, and the widespread culture of hatred in Vietnamese internet communities. It shows the need for empathy, critical thinking, and responsible online engagement in shaping our digital culture. As we continue to grapple with the implication of the digital age, it's important to know the dangers posed by the culture of digital lynching.

Wider implications

This op-ed focuses more on the "public" than the "policy" side of the journal. While policy changes can help, the most important factor is the public's awareness and responsibility. Current Vietnamese policies on digital spaces are unclear and need improvement, as they don't address harmful content or provide support for victims of online harassment.
