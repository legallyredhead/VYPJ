Trade liberalization is generally argued to lead to both an improvement in economic efficiency when countries concentrate on their comparative advantages and to a rise in overall growth as the size of the market expands, evidently through relative price changes and corresponding increases in real wages for consumers (Muendler, 2017).

Yet, in February 2018, the United States issued massive trade restrictions towards China, its biggest trading partner, with the pretext of softening the trade deficits - an uncharacteristic protectionist policy that goes against the commonplace benefits of trade liberalization.

This unprecedented trade action raised many questions. How has the U.S. economy been hurt, especially in Chinese-reliant industries like manufacturing and high-tech? How have these impacts translated for average consumers, as reflected in their real wages and inflation levels. How this translated into like impacts for normal consumers, reflected in their real wages, inflation levels?

Overall, the US Economy is worse off

Most of the economic literature predicts that trade wars will damage growth, income distribution and employment in all countries (Erken et al., 2017, and 2018; Bollen and Rojas-Romagosa, 2018; UNCTAD, 2018).

The scale of the tariffs towards China was vast, encompassing significant portions of the U.S.'s total imports and exports – approximately $350 billion of imports from China and, as a retaliatory measure, around $100 billion of U.S. exports were targeted. Cumulatively, the tariffs targeted imports and exports amounting to 3.6% of US GDP, hurting all sectors from manufacturing, agriculture, to electronics and energy.

Contrary to the objectives, the trade war did not reverse the trade imbalances. Instead, it resulted in lower US GDP growth, welfare, and employment. Caliendo and Parro (2022) find aggregate manufacturing imports declined by 6.5 percent and manufacturing exports by about 8 percent as a result of the trade war. Estimated costs to the US economy range from $6.9–7.2 billion by the end of 2018 (Fajgelbaum et al., 2020), with the trade war costing US firms $1.7 trillion in market capitalization (Amiti et al., 2019) and reducing investment growth by 1.9 percentage points in 2020 (Amiti et al., 2020).

US exports to China fell by 18% from 2017 to 2019, while imports fell by 11% over the same period. This resulted in an estimated peak loss of 245,000 jobs in the US and real household income being $88 billion lower over 2018–2019 (in 2020 prices), or around $675 per household. The extensive tariffs led to a reconfiguration of supply chains, especially in Chinese-reliant industries like electronics, machinery, motor vehicles, experienced heightened production costs, and forced many leading US firms to seek alternative, oftentimes more expensive sources for materials.

US firms are bearing the brunt of increased costs and policy uncertainty

The extensive tariffs led to a drastic reconfiguration of supply chains, especially in Chinese-reliant industries like electronics, machinery, motor vehicles, that led to heightened production costs. Handley, Kamal, and Monarch's 2020 study reveals that the firms affected by the tariff changes make up a significant U.S. economy, accounting for 84 percent of U.S. exports and 65 percent of manufacturing employment. Complementing this, Flaaen and Pierce's 2019 research highlights that while U.S. manufacturing industries did receive some protection from the increased tariffs, this was counterbalanced by greater negative impacts due to rising input costs and retaliatory tariffs. Furthermore, Amiti et al. (2019) estimated that trade diversion due to these tariffs accounted for approximately USD 165 billion in 2018 alone, including USD 136 billion in US imports and USD 29 billion in US exports, imposing significant costs on multinational enterprises. Fajgelbaum et al. (2019) suggest that these producer and consumer losses amount to approximately USD 68.8 billion per year or 0.4% of U.S. GDP.

Particularly impacted by the bilateral restrictions is the U.S. technology sector, home to giants like Intel and NVIDIA, which are integral to the burgeoning technology competitive edge of the U.S. Economy. According to Counterpoint Research, the restrictions are expected to affect only about 10% of China's logic chip production through 2025. However, for the U.S, these restrictions have led to a drastic reduction in semiconductor exports to China, with a 50.7% drop to $3.1 billion in the first eight months of 2023, compared to $6.4 billion during the same period in 2022. This decline highlights the vulnerability of U.S. semiconductor supply chains, heavily reliant on Chinese production of critical minerals like gallium and germanium as China accounts for 53 percent of the United States’ supply of raw gallium metal and 54 percent of its germanium imports, essential for high-performance semiconductors.

Prices increases borne primarily by consumers:

When import tariffs are imposed, a loss in deadweight and welfare occurs due to a shift to less efficient market outcomes as the participants are forced to waste or underutilize resources.

Indeed, the most important channel of protectionist trade measures would run through price effects, impacting households (workers) through the real wage channel. A key aspect of the tariffs' impact is on prices borne by consumers. Research by Cavallo, Gopinath, Neiman, and Tang, using Bureau of Labor Statistics data, indicates that tariff revenue was largely absorbed by US importers, leading to a significant increase in post-tariff import prices borne primarily by consumers. This effect is corroborated by Amiti et al. (2018), who found that the full burden of increased tariffs fell on US consumers, resulting in a monthly reduction of real income by USD 1.4 billion, in addition to USD 3 billion in added tax costs monthly.

On average, real wages fell by 1%, but this varied greatly, with a standard deviation of 0.5% across counties. This variation is linked to regional specialization: while the Great Lakes region and the Northeast, known for industrial activity, received higher tariff protection, agricultural areas like the Midwestern plains and Mountain West were more affected by China's retaliatory tariffs, which targeted about 27% of U.S. agricultural goods.

Heterogeneous impacts on real wages and consumptions ability:

Research by Michael Waugh found that counties that were more exposed to Chinese retaliatory tariffs experienced a reduced ability to export, which fed into the labor market (both tradable and non-tradable), and this mechanism reduced consumption. This disparity in economic impact is further illustrated in consumer behavior, with a marked decline in auto sales in counties more exposed to tariffs. High-tariff counties experienced a 2 to 5.5 percentage point reduction in auto sales growth compared to low-tariff counties, according to county-level analysis.

Clearly, the impact of job losses and reduced wages post-tariffs tend to be geographically localized and limited to certain sectors and occupations, affecting the already worse-off states and Americans who are structurally disadvantaged in the labor market. The trade war's harmful effects, such as increased unemployment, lower wages, and reduced labor force participation, have disproportionately impacted manufacturing workers (Autor et al., 2013, 2014; Pierce and Schott, 2016a), individuals in lower-income brackets (Chetverikov et al., 2015), and the less educated (Greenland and Lopresti, 2016). These demographic groups have faced not only economic challenges but also declines in physical and mental health (McManus and Schaur, 2016), and an increase in mortality rates (Pierce and Schott, 2016b). Furthermore, Feler and Senses (2017) highlight the broader societal impacts, such as reduced tax revenues leading to decreased government budgets and public services.

Trade war's impact extends far beyond mere trade figures, deepening pre-existing U.S. inequalities. The localized nature of job losses and wage reductions in high-tariff counties has disproportionately impacted those in lower-income brackets and less educated demographics. Such a widening gap underscores a critical aspect of trade conflicts: they often hit those who are least equipped to absorb the shock, thereby contributing to a cycle of increasing inequality.

Policies recommendations:

Implementing phased tariff reductions between the US and China can substantially improve economic outcomes. A proposed reduction of 7.5% on $112 billion worth of imports from China in 2021, followed by a further 12.5% reduction on $250 billion worth of imports in 2022, can significantly enhance US GDP by 2025. This strategy should be accompanied by reinforcing economic cooperation, particularly in addressing global economic challenges and reinvigorating trade agreements. Fostering this cooperative stance can lead to mutual economic growth and stability, benefiting industries from agriculture to high-tech sectors, including semiconductors. Escalating tariffs, such as imposing a 45% tariff on all Chinese imports, would have detrimental effects on the U.S. economy.

In protecting strategic industries, the US should adopt a strategy that safeguards sectors like semiconductors and AI without resorting to broad economic decoupling with China. This involves enhancing measures for cybersecurity and intellectual property rights protection, while still encouraging technological exchange and cooperation with China. Such a strategy can help maintain the competitive edge of US high-tech firms, like Intel and NVIDIA, in the global market. Additionally, it is vital to promote research and development within these sectors to ensure continuous innovation and to mitigate any negative impacts from trade restrictions. This balanced approach not only secures key national interests but also fosters a conducive environment for technological and economic advancement.
