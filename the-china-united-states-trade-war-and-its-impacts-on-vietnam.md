The China - United States Trade War and its Impacts on Vietnam and the World

In 2018, an economic concern loomed in the White House of the United States of America - the country was at its largest trade deficit to ever be recorded. With Donald Trump holding the position of the country’s President, he had redirected his focus onto such a deficit. The reason for this was somewhat clear to Trump: a few years before that, the U.S. and China had engaged in high levels of bilateral trade, and by 2018, U.S. imports from China far exceeded its exports. Trump, single-mindedly aiming toward American economic success, accused China of participating in unethical and one-sided trade practices and said that America had been “taken total advantage of” (White House Archive, 2018). Following a series of threats, on March 22nd and July 18th, 2018, Trump signed a memorandum to impose tariffs on Chinese products. Specifically, $50 billion worth of Chinese products were placed under a 25% American tariff in the first round and an additional $34 billion of such products in the second (York, 2024).

In retaliation, China implemented its own tariffs on American goods, which indeed took a toll on the American trade industry as the U.S. and China were close economic partners. At that time, China was an economically ambitious nation. With Xi Jinping taking the position of the General Secretary of the Communist Party, China no longer wanted to hide itself and its potential. Instead, the country was eyeing the leading spot in the world economy stage, and it was indeed succeeding in its quest with multiple governmental plans aiming to strengthen the country’s already vastly growing economy as well as to globalize its economic influence.

This was the start of a series of tit-for-tat rounds of tariffs and retaliations from both the U.S. and China, neither wanting to back down; thus, resulting in a swiftly escalating trade tension between the 2 countries, straining their strategic partnership. The economic result was in no way positive either, as, on the one hand, the U.S. trade deficit even increased after the commencement of the trade war. On the other hand, China also lost a supplier of various agricultural products. In 2020, U.S. President Trump and General Secretary Jinping signed the Phase One Deal, committing to the mending and renewal of the two country’s trade relations. However, the deal failed to alleviate all the effects of the trade war on the two countries’ relations, supplies, and job force. Even then, the conflict continued to advance during the Biden administration, with tariffs placed on Chinese technological products, slowing down China’s technology sector. “Decoupling” was also a main concern in this era, as the U.S. and its allies sought to divest from Chinese sources to other partners such as ASEAN nations.

Due to such goals, the U.S.-China trade war has had profound effects on the global economy. The United States, once the leading force of economic globalization and the firm supporter of international trade organizations such as WTO is now the one country to create more trade barriers and divide amongst nations. China’s position as an economic powerhouse after the 2008 financial crisis is also threatened, and the global economy is facing the risk of an economic recession due to this war.

Moreover, global security and the current political standing are also likely to be changed in accordance with this war. Trump not only imposed economic limitations on China but also on other nations such as Japan, Mexico, and the EU bloc, many of which are U.S. allies. This means that America’s relationship with these countries is drastically dwindling, allowing opponents such as Russia, and, naturally, China to gain more support and trade partners from these allies. Such shifts in economic alliances are an indicator of impending changes in the political atmosphere as well as potential clashes in strategies, resulting in an even more competitive race among a wide variety of nations. In addition, such polarization could even result in activities beyond sanctions and tariffs.

In response to such a situation, Vietnam opts for the neutral approach, as does its bloc, ASEAN. It is important to note that both sides of the trade war are worried about an enhanced influence of its opponent in this region, however, ASEAN has acted in a way that is most economically beneficial to them: not taking any preference, while accepting investment interest from foreign economies. This means opportunities are opening up for ASEAN’s economic development, especially for Vietnam, since the foreign direct investment (FDI) funds are being shifted from China to the country, promoting growth in fundamental sectors such as Technology and Automobile. Moreover, the reduction in trade between the U.S., China, and their allies created potential opportunities for Vietnam to become a more prominent market for global imports and exports, playing a stronger role as a trade partner to China, the United States, and other countries as well.

However, the trade war has obvious negative impacts on ASEAN and Vietnam in particular, both economically and diplomatically. For starters, the escalation of the conflict and the involvement of more and more countries due to decoupling means that it is getting increasingly more difficult to remain in a neutral position while maintaining good trade relations with the two sides. This dilemma is manifested in the form of economic projects, as ASEAN countries find it hard to participate in Chinese or American-led economic development plans, as the risk of upsetting either side is huge on the economy of the bloc as a whole. The situation is even more tender with Vietnam, as China is Vietnam’s long-standing partner, not just economically but in other aspects as well, whereas the United States and Vietnam have made great strides in mending their diplomatic relationship post-Cold War. To maintain such positive relationships and cooperative partnerships, Vietnam must be extremely cautious and smart about its move to prevent one-sided involvement that can alter the dynamic of the political situation. Moreover, the trade war also means that the economy of the two most important trade partners of Vietnam - the U.S. and China - will be shrinking, thus, reducing demands for Vietnam’s exports as a whole, as well as the amount of money invested in Vietnam. An even bigger concern can be raised over the fact that Vietnam is at risk of U.S. tariff at practically any time now, and it is being even more thoroughly scrutinized since being decided by America as one of the most important trade partners, replacing China’s previous position. If such an event occurs, it will surely damage the Vietnamese market even further.

In the future, the U.S.-China trade war will possibly escalate even further, or at least expand into new areas, leaving Vietnam in a more risky and vulnerable state. Though there are certain benefits and opportunities for the Vietnamese economy yielded by such an event, it is important that Vietnam thoroughly devises its reaction plans to any possible advancement of the conflict and acts swiftly and appropriately to prevent further financial damages.
