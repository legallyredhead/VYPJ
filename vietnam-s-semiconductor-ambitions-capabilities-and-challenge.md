Industry Outlook:

The global semiconductor industry is experiencing rapid growth, driven by increasing demand for advanced technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), autonomous vehicles, and 5G connectivity. This surge has led to heightened competition among nations to establish themselves as leaders in chip production. Vietnam is projected to see its high-tech export revenue reach $135 billion by 2024, accounting for over 30% of total exports, positioning it as a major player in ASEAN. With ambitions to move further up the tech value chain, the Vietnamese government has set ambitious targets, aiming to train at least 50,000 chip engineers and designers by 2030 to meet the rising global demand.

Vietnam's strategic location and cost advantages make it a prime candidate for further growth. The increasing appeal of Vietnam’s talent is largely driven by the global surge in demand for chip engineers, spurred by the AI boom and supply chain shifts caused by U.S.-China tensions. The country aims to capitalize on these trends by attracting more investments and solidifying its place in the semiconductor sector.

World-wide semiconductors “race"

Nations worldwide are launching comprehensive strategies to secure their positions in the global semiconductor market:

European Union: The Digital Compass Plan aims to produce 20% of the global semiconductor output by 2030, with a focus on increasing manufacturing value by 6-44%. Incentives such as 50% R&D tax credits and projected investments between $20 to $35 billion over the next decade are encouraging growth.

South Korea: The K-Belt National Chip Plan seeks to create a "K-semiconductor belt," doubling its workforce and securing a leading position in chip production by 2030. Supported by a $55 to $65 billion budget over three years, the plan offers tax credits of 25% and other incentives.

China: As part of its 14th Five-Year Plan, China aims for chip self-sufficiency by 2025. With national and local funds totaling $100 billion, alongside tax exemptions and state financing initiatives, China is investing heavily in R&D to bolster its capabilities.

These global policy shifts reflect a drive to secure semiconductor supply chains, highlighting the strategic importance of the sector amid geopolitical concerns. Vietnam's proactive efforts to build its tech sector offer opportunities to attract investments from companies looking to diversify away from other major hubs.

Dissecting Vietnam's potential to participate in this semiconductor “rush"

Vietnam has several strengths that contribute to its emerging role in the semiconductor industry. Its favorable geographical location, stable political environment, and strong electronics industry are assets. However, there are also challenges and opportunities that must be considered.

Strengths:

Vietnam has a young, skilled, and high-quality workforce, with over 70 universities focusing on STEM. Companies like Samsung, Intel, LG, and Foxconn have established significant operations, helping to build a foundation for further growth. Vietnamese firms such as FPT and Vingroup are also expanding their technology capabilities

.Vietnam has a stable political environment, favorable logistics, and a young, skilled workforce. Over 50% of the population is under 30, creating a "golden demographic period" that is expected to last until 2039. With around 1.8 million university graduates annually, Vietnam has a robust talent pool, many of whom specialize in science and technology. The government has pledged to train 50,000 semiconductor engineers by 2030, further bolstering the workforce. Vietnam's relatively low labor costs (ranging from $3,100 to $3,800 per person per year as of 2023) make it an attractive option for foreign companies looking to establish cost-effective production facilities.

Vietnam's strategic location also enhances its appeal. Situated in the heart of Southeast Asia, it offers easy access to major markets such as Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia. Its proximity to China and India allows Vietnam to serve as a bridge for companies looking to reach these vast markets. Additionally, Vietnam’s position in the global supply chain is being strengthened through strategic partnerships with the U.S., Japan, South Korea, and others, with recent agreements focusing on high-tech collaboration and talent development.

Weaknesses:

Despite its advantages, Vietnam faces significant challenges, including infrastructure constraints. According to the Global Innovation Index 2023, Vietnam ranks 71st out of 132 countries in infrastructure, reflecting ongoing issues with transportation and logistics. While there have been improvements, Vietnam still grapples with congested roads, underdeveloped rail networks, and limited port capacity, increasing costs and reducing efficiency. Additionally, Vietnam's R&D capabilities remain limited. The country spends only 0.42% of its GDP on R&D, which is lower than that of developed semiconductor nations like South Korea and the U.S., constraining its ability to innovate and compete globally.

Another critical challenge is the need for better integration into the global tech ecosystem. Vietnam's companies often struggle to access advanced technologies due to high costs and legal complexities, impeding their ability to develop competitive products. Furthermore, Vietnam's education system is still catching up to the demands of the tech sector, producing fewer STEM graduates than required to sustain growth in the semiconductor industry.

Opportunities:

There is significant foreign interest in Vietnam’s semiconductor sector. Companies like Alchip Technologies plan to expand their engineering staff, specializing in AI chip design. Synopsys has positioned a team of 500 engineers to lead advancements in chiplet and multi-die chip design. Marvell aims to make Vietnam its third-largest global hub for chip design, growing its workforce to around 500 by 2026. Additionally, South Korean firms are converting their Vietnamese branches into major R&D centers, particularly for AI chips in autonomous vehicles. These companies are investing in local talent through scholarships, internships, and collaborations with universities, ensuring a steady supply of skilled engineers. These moves could help Vietnam realize its ambition of moving up the tech value chain.

During President Biden's visit in September 2023, many companies listed above committed to expanding operations in Vietnam, focusing on Assembly, Testing, and Packaging (ATP)—a crucial sector of the semiconductor supply chain where Vietnam can capitalize on its competitive costs and skilled labor.

Vietnam's reserves of rare earth elements, the second-largest globally, further enhance its appeal. These minerals are essential for producing high-tech products, from semiconductors to electric vehicle batteries. Vietnam has already signed agreements with countries like South Korea to develop a rare earth supply chain, securing an essential position in the global tech industry.

Threats:

Vietnam’s rapid growth in the semiconductor sector could be hampered by serious infrastructural limitations and rising competition from other countries like India and Thailand, which offer lower labor costs or larger markets. The competition is fierce, with countries worldwide racing to develop their semiconductor capabilities and attract investment. Vietnam must address these challenges quickly, as noted by Deputy Minister Trần Duy Đông, who emphasized the need for rapid infrastructure improvements and policy reform within the next 24 months to ensure competitiveness.

Additionally, Vietnam's ability to sustain growth depends on its capacity to enhance its R&D capabilities. Without substantial investments in innovation, Vietnam risks becoming a low-cost manufacturing hub rather than an integrated player in the global tech ecosystem.

Current regulatory movements within the industry in Vietnam

The global race in the semiconductor industry sees top companies actively investing in Vietnam. Examples include:

Samsung's two mobile phone manufacturing plants in Bac Ninh and Thai Nguyen.

Intel's chip assembly, packaging, and testing plant in Ho Chi Minh City.

Amkor’s semiconductor manufacturing facility, worth $1.6 billion, in Bac Ninh (2023).

Micron Vina’s semiconductor plant, valued at $1 billion, in Bac Giang (2023).

Companies such as Renesas, Qorvo, Marvell, Synopsys, Ampere, and Infineon are also involved in large-scale R&D for integrated circuit (IC) design in Vietnam. Additionally, Google, Amazon, and Nvidia are providing AI training courses in the country.

Artificial Intelligence Development Strategy:

Vietnam’s AI development strategy, outlined in Decision No. 127/QD-TTG, aims to position AI as a significant technological field by 2030. The goal is to make Vietnam a center for innovation and development of AI solutions, contributing to a creative society, an efficient government, national security, and sustainable economic growth.

Key strategic directions include:

Administrative reforms and the development of a legal framework.

Financial investment support.

Tax and fiscal policy adjustments.

Assistance for small and medium-sized enterprises.

Promotion of open manufacturing.

Coordination and monitoring mechanisms for implementation.

Vietnam's Semiconductor Industry Growth Plan: Vietnam’s plan for the semiconductor industry is divided into three phases:

Phase 1: Annual revenue over $25 billion, with local added value reaching 10-15%. The goal is to establish 100 design companies, 1 manufacturing company, and 10 semiconductor packaging and testing firms, supported by a workforce of over 50,000 engineers.

Phase 2: Annual revenue over $50 billion, with local added value increasing to 15-20%. Expansion includes 200 design companies, 5 manufacturing companies, and 20 packaging and testing firms.

Phase 3: Annual revenue exceeding $75 billion, with local added value at 20-25%. By this phase, Vietnam aims to develop self-sufficient semiconductor production capabilities with 300 design companies, 5 manufacturers, and 20 packaging and testing firms.

Human Resources Development in the Semiconductor Industry:

By 2030, Vietnam plans to train 50,000 engineers for the semiconductor industry and an additional 35,000 for other roles. This includes around 15,000 design engineers and the establishment of 18 national-standard laboratories across the country. The strategy involves setting up 4 national-level labs across the northern, central, and southern regions and expanding training to around 200 locations.

Foreign corporations and Semiconductors

The Vietnamese government has initiated comprehensive policies to support the semiconductor industry. Deputy Minister Trần Duy Đông noted that high-level meetings between the Vietnamese Prime Minister and U.S. partners emphasized the need for rapid implementation, particularly focusing on three core areas:

Completion of a competitive regulatory framework to ensure Vietnam can attract and retain investments.

Synchronized infrastructure upgrades in electricity, water, transportation, fiber optics, and IT to meet the demands of high-tech industries.

Development of a high-quality workforce, including investment in education, retraining, and collaboration with international firms.

The government’s strategy is further articulated through the “Development of Semiconductor Industry Workforce until 2030, Vision to 2045” plan. This proposal, developed by the Ministry of Planning and Investment in collaboration with other governmental bodies, seeks to build a comprehensive ecosystem that includes training programs, R&D, infrastructure development, and international partnerships. It identifies several key tasks:

Training high-quality engineers: Increasing collaboration between universities, research institutes, and private enterprises to ensure the workforce meets the industry's needs.

Attracting global talent and expertise: Encouraging the return of Vietnamese professionals working abroad in the semiconductor field.

Encouraging Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs): Supporting infrastructure development through PPPs and creating incentives for private investments.

Enhancing international cooperation: Strengthening ties with leading countries in the semiconductor industry, like the U.S., to benefit from technology transfer, joint ventures, and strategic alliances.

Deputy Minister Đông emphasized that Vietnam could not progress alone; the country must engage in swift and comprehensive cooperation with global leaders in the semiconductor sector. In doing so, Vietnam can "stand on the shoulders of giants," leveraging its demographic advantages and strategic initiatives to emerge as a formidable player in the semiconductor industry.

The government has identified seven critical tasks for implementing its workforce development plan:

Training formal education lecturers and students, as well as short-term courses to meet immediate industry demands.

Boosting R&D efforts across national labs and private sectors.

Investing in technology infrastructure, including specialized facilities for semiconductor research and production.

Attracting international experts and professionals.

Supporting job creation and the growth of semiconductor enterprises.

Crafting specific policies to enhance the training and development of the semiconductor workforce.

Promoting international collaboration and creating awareness about Vietnam’s growing semiconductor industry.
