OP-ED: Sexual Abuse: When Legal Prejudices Leave Children Behind

FINDING AN IDEAL VICTIMS

A United Nations study on gender stereotypes in the laws and court decisions in Southeast Asia notes that misconceptions about who a "real" or "ideal" victim is worsen criminal justice officers' perceptions of how an "ideal" victim should behave. For instance, findings from Vietnam show that criminal justice officers believe a "real" victim needs to show extreme fear, helplessness, or terror or have a state of panic. A victim who recounts their abuse calmly or coldly is not seen as behaving appropriately and their story might be considered unreliable.

In Vietnam, this narrative of finding the “right” victim is especially problematic when it comes to cases involving young boys. This oversight stems from entrenched gender stereotypes that paint men as the strong, active participants in sexual activity, thereby making it harder for boys to disclose abuse. This stigma is further compounded by societal taboos surrounding same-sex relations, especially in cultures where homosexuality is frowned upon.

Boys sexually abused by women might remain silent due to the societal belief that men cannot be victims. When boys are abused by men, the situation becomes even more complex due to the prevailing misconceptions about same-sex relations. These stereotypes not only force boys into silence but also lead to less sympathy and support compared to female victims.

Therefore, it is essential to review and revise laws to recognize all victims of abuse, regardless of gender by setting specific definitions. All victims are victims, and their experiences and behaviors in the aftermath of abuse should not be judged based on preconceived notions of what an "ideal" victim looks like.

ABANDONED VICTIMS

The child protection system in Vietnam is riddled with inadequacies. Minister of Public Security To Lam, during a National Assembly session in June 2018, proposed a special handling process for child sexual abuse cases. Yet, no concrete solution has been implemented. This lack of action leaves many victims feeling abandoned by the very system meant to protect them.

The dropout rate at the reporting stage is alarmingly high. Victims often face multiple recounts of their traumatic experiences, disrespectful treatment, and a lack of sensitivity from authorities. Dropout can occur at various stages of the criminal justice process: reporting, investigation, prosecution, and trial. The dropout rate at the reporting and initial contact stage is very high. Many victims are abandoned and “encouraged” towards reconciliation or other forms of resolution outside of formal judicial procedures. Victims often have to recount their story multiple times and are treated disrespectfully and insensitively, even though sexual abuse is one of the most severe physical and psychological violations against children.

If the family chooses to report the case to the police and pursue the criminal justice process (investigation, prosecution, and trial), the child will have to recount their story multiple times, each time potentially reliving the traumatic events, thus becoming a victim again. Girls/female victims may be subjected to multiple rigorous, invasive, and disrespectful physical examinations.

It is essential to apply appropriate and flexible measures, taking into account all aspects to protect the rights of children. Victims should have the right to refuse requests for questioning, taking statements, or direct contact or confrontation with the suspect, or any other person representing the suspect.

A case is dismissed when authorities decide not to pursue it, and it is deemed non-existent if the police fail to seriously investigate, cannot identify the suspect, or if the victim withdraws their complaint. In assessing voluntary withdrawal from victims, it is crucial to recognize that early negative assessments by the police about prosecution challenges can discourage victims. This indicates a need for systemic reforms to ensure that all reported cases of abuse are thoroughly investigated and pursued without bias. To address this, law enforcement and prosecutorial bodies must adopt more supportive approaches, with enhanced training for officials and improved support systems for victims. There should also be investigation relating to early case dismissals and premature withdrawals.

Another unreasonable aspect of criminal investigation of child sexual abuse cases in Vietnam is the rigid application of the principle of evidence over testimony, due to stereotypes about age and the truthfulness of children’s statements, without considering psychological factors, methods, and the child's rights during evidence provision. This results in cases with weak evidence often ending in suspended sentences to avoid large compensation claims for wrongful imprisonment, or lighter sentences within the lowest penalty range.

FAILS OF CHILD PROTECTION AGENCY

Vietnam’s child protection framework involves 15 different agencies and organizations, from the People's Courts to the Ho Chi Minh Communist Youth Union. Despite this extensive network, the system often fails to provide adequate protection for children. Policies intended to protect children are frequently seen as mere programs chasing annual targets rather than providing sustainable solutions.

A child protection system is essential not just for addressing current cases but also for preventing future abuse. The 2015 and 2017 amendments to the Penal Code have been steps in the right direction, but continuous adjustments are needed to ensure that the law effectively protects all children. Society's assumptions can be flawed. Public anger can quickly shift to new cases. The measures of justice standards can be skewed. Only the trauma remains with the victims.
