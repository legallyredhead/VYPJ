Breaking the Cycle: Rethinking Corporal Punishment Norms for Children’s Rights in Vietnam

Abstract

This paper delves into the deeply ingrained norms of corporal punishment in Vietnam while examining the effectiveness of existing policies. Through a thorough exploration of cultural, societal, and legal facets, this unravels the complex factors sustaining the prevalence of physical discipline among parents and educators.

Introduction

In today's rapidly changing world, where conversations about human rights and child welfare are gaining momentum, the persistence of corporal punishment norms in Vietnam stands as a stark contradiction. In Vietnam, nearly 70% of children have experienced violence at home from their parents and caregivers, ranking 27th out of 75 countries (UNICEF, 2023). For Vietnamese youth, corporal punishment isn't just a relic of the past; it's a lived experience that shapes their perceptions of authority, self-worth, and safety.

Corporal punishment, defined as the use of physical force intended to cause pain or discomfort as a form of discipline or control, remains prevalent among parents and teachers. The norms “yêu cho roi cho vọt, ghét cho ngọt cho bùi” ("spare the rod, spoil the child") echoes through generations, with the belief that physical discipline is a necessary tool to foster discipline and respect in children. However, as we dive deeper into the repercussions of such practices, it becomes evident that this practice comes at the cost of children's physical and psychological well-being.

This paper will dissect the multifaceted issue surrounding corporal punishment norms in Vietnam through the lens of children's rights. In addition to cultural and social aspects, the study will examine the existing legal framework governing corporal punishment and evaluete its effectiveness of Children’s Law of Vietnam. This paper aim to shed light on its impact on Vietnamese youth and advocate for a shift towards non-violent disciplinary practices, paving the way for a future where every child in Vietnam grows up in an environment that nurtures their potential and safeguards their rights.

Issue Analysis

Corporal punishment, deeply ingrained in Vietnamese culture, remains a contentious issue with significant implications for children's rights and well-being. Its roots can be traced back to traditional beliefs that physical discipline is essential for instilling respect and obedience in children (Smith, 2018). Despite modernization efforts, corporal punishment persists in Vietnamese households and schools, reflecting broader societal attitudes towards discipline and authority.

The 1992 Constitution (amended and supplemented in 2013) has institutionalised views on protecting human rights. On the basis of these Constitutional rights, the right of children to be free from violence are clearly stated. The law prohibits acts that harm children, and explains very clearly what acts of violence against children (prohibiting physical punishment), yet there are still various incidents that hurt and harm children both in the family and at school continued to occur on television and newspapers: “1st grade students had their fingers broken by the teacher”, “father threw his daughter out into the yard”, etc. And most of the time, this is trenched in the widespread acceptance and normalisation of corporal punishment.

The current status quo of corporal punishment in Vietnam is characterized by a complex interplay of cultural traditions. Vietnam, deeply rooted in Eastern Culture, places strong emphasis on filial piety, valuing resếpct for parents and elders. Within this cultural framework, parent-child relationships often embody authoritarian dynamics, where children feel obligated to fulfil their parents' expectations and follow their decisions. This societal norm is expressed in the popular proverb “Yêu cho roi cho vọt, ghét cho ngọt cho bùi", or a similar one in English, “He who withholds his rod hates his son, But he who loves him disciplines him diligently". This suggests an acceptance of corporal punishment as a form of discipline, or even out of love, in Vietnam.

Within Vietnamese society, physical punishment is also deeply entrenched in familial and educational practices. Parents often resort to physical discipline as a means of correcting perceived misbehaviour, viewing it as a form of tough love and necessary for character development (Nguyen, 2017). Similarly, teachers employ corporal punishment in schools to maintain discipline and order.

The broader context of corporal punishment extends beyond individual disciplinary practices, reflecting broader power dynamics and social norms. Gender roles and cultural expectations further perpetuate the normalisation of corporal punishment, with boys more likely to experience physical discipline than girls (UNICEF, 2021). Moreover, socio-economic disparities exacerbate the issue, as children from marginalised communities are disproportionately affected by corporal punishment due to limited access to resources and support systems (Tuoitrevn, 2020). “This is just the tip of the iceberg, because in Vietnam there are still many unreported cases," Ms. Lesley Miller - deputy chief representative of the United Nations Children's Fund in Vietnam (Unicef) - emphasised at the meeting.

Data and reports highlight the prevalence and impact of corporal punishment on Vietnamese youth. A study conducted by UNICEF revealed that over 70% of Vietnamese children experience some form of corporal punishment at home or in school (UNICEF, 2021). Out of every 10 children surveyed, more than 6 children have been subjected to psychological punishment and 4 children have been physically punished. Mr. Dang Hoa Nam, Director of the Department of Children (Ministry of Labor, War Invalids and Social Affairs) admitted: "We are not too surprised by the fact that nearly 71% of children have been educated through violent punishment."

Policy intervention is imperative to address the critical aspects of corporal punishment in Vietnam. Strengthening enforcement mechanisms and raising awareness about children's rights are essential steps towards creating a protective environment for Vietnamese youth.

Impact Assessment

The effects of corporal punishment on Vietnamese youth are profound and far-reaching. Beyond physical harm, children subjected to corporal punishment often experience psychological trauma, eroding their sense of self-worth and security (Le & Vu, 2019). Research indicates that children who experience corporal punishment are more likely to develop aggression, anxiety, and depression, affecting their overall well-being and future prospects (Tran & Nguyen, 2018).

The immediate implications of corporal punishment extend to children's educational experiences, with studies showing a negative correlation between physical discipline and academic achievement. Fear of punishment may hinder children's engagement in learning activities, perpetuating a cycle of underachievement and disengagement from school (Pham & Le, 2020).

In the long term, the impact of corporal punishment on Vietnamese youth can have lasting consequences on their socio-emotional development and societal integration. Children who experience corporal punishment may internalize violent behaviors and attitudes, perpetuating cycles of violence in future generations. Moreover, the normalization of corporal punishment perpetuates inequalities and reinforces power dynamics within Vietnamese society, hindering efforts towards social cohesion and inclusive development (Tran, 2019).

Addressing the issue of corporal punishment in Vietnam requires a multifaceted approach that considers its cultural, social, and legal dimensions. By recognizing the broader context of corporal punishment and its impact on Vietnamese youth, policymakers can enact meaningful reforms that prioritize children's rights and well-being.

Need for Policy Change:

The current legal framework concerning children's rights and protection in Vietnam, particularly regarding corporal punishment, presents notable inadequacies warranting immediate attention. While the Vietnamese government has ratified international treaties advocating for children's rights, including the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), and set out a domestic framework in “Children’s Law”, it is clear that implementation remains uneven (Ministry of Labor, Invalids and Social Affairs, 2020). Enforcement mechanisms are lacking, leading to widespread acceptance and normalization of corporal punishment.

Analyzing the current law reveals specific strengths and shortcomings that render it ineffective in adequately addressing the issue of corporal punishment. Firstly, while the Law on Child Protection, Care, and Education prohibits physical punishment, and it actually provide clear definitions and guidelines on what constitute as violence against children, being “the act of tormenting, mistreating, and beating; harming body and health; insulting and insulting honor and dignity; isolation, exclusion and other intentional acts that cause physical and mental harm to children.” Yet, a significant gap exists between the intent of policies and their actual implementation. The interpretation of violence against children and corporal punishment varies widely among individuals: Many believe these are different. This ambiguity creates challenges in effectively enforcing and prosecuting cases of abuse. It also leads to inconsistencies in addressing instances of corporal punishment. The law lacks stringent enforcement and mechanisms to ensure compliance and accountability among parents, educators, and caregivers.

Another pressing issue is the widespread lack of awareness and understanding among the general population regarding the provisions of the law and children's rights. Many parents and teachers are not familiar with alternative disciplinary methods or the detrimental effects of corporal punishment on children's well-being. Cultural and societal norms further worsen this problem by perpetuating the acceptance of physical punishment in Vietnamese households and schools. Traditional beliefs emphasizing strict discipline and respect for authority figures, normalizing physical punishment. There is also a glaring absence of adequate education and training on positive parenting practices and non-violent disciplinary techniques for parents and caregivers. Without such education, they may continue to rely on corporal discipline as a method of behavior correction. Additionally, the lackluster education system contributes to children not reporting instances of corporal punishment, hence many incidents may go unreported or be culturally justified, thereby hindering efforts to effectively address the issue.

Policy Recommendations:

To effectively address the deficiencies in current policies and combat the issue of corporal punishment in Vietnam, a comprehensive set of innovative and practical policy recommendations is essential:

Strengthen legal frameworks by enacting comprehensive legislation explicitly prohibiting corporal punishment in all settings. Clear and unequivocal guidance on the rights and protection of children against all forms of violence is indispensable for ensuring their safety and well-being.

Strengthen enforcement mechanisms to ensure compliance with existing laws prohibiting corporal punishment. This entails implementing regular monitoring and supervision protocols in schools and households to detect and address instances of physical discipline promptly.

Promote positive discipline practices. This involves developing and disseminating guidelines and training programs for parents, educators, and caregivers on non-violent disciplinary techniques. This can help shift societal norms towards more compassionate and effective approaches to discipline.

Enhance support services for families at risk of resorting to corporal punishment. Expanding access to counseling, support groups, or at least contact information to equip paảents and children with the necessary skills and reéources.

Incoporate children's voices into policy-making processes. Ensuring children's active participation in decisions related to policies and programs affecting their well-being can foster a sense of ownership and empowerment. Creating platforms for children to express their views and advocate for their rights is essential for promoting a culture of respect and dignity.

Implementing these policy recommendations has the potential to significantly impact the lives of Vietnamese youth by creating safer, more nurturing environments conducive to their growth and development.

As young individuals, we hold a special power to bring about social change and impact policy decisions. We can spread awareness about the detrimental effects of corporal punishment and by advocating for non-violent disciplinary approaches, we can significantly influence the attitudes and behaviors within our communities. Getting involved in local initiatives, organizing awareness-raising events, and working alongside stakeholders can magnify our voices and rally support for policy reforms. We can actually also contribute to policy reforms, either indirectly by getting involved in local initiatives, organizing awareness-raising event or directly.

Conclusion

The issue of corporal punishment in Vietnam presents complex challenges rooted in cultural norms, legal ambiguities, and societal attitudes. Despite existing laws prohibiting physical discipline, enforcement remains inadequate, leading to widespread acceptance and normalization of corporal punishment in homes and schools. The detrimental effects of corporal punishment on children's well-being urge the need for policy reforms that prioritize non-violent disciplinary practices and promote children's inherent rights.

The proposed policy changes outlined in this paper offer tangible solutions to address the shortcomings of current legislation and effectively combat the issue of corporal punishment. Strengthening enforcement mechanisms, promoting positive parenting practices, enhancing support services, and incorporating children's voices in policy-making processes - These are essential steps towards creating a safer and more nurturing environment for Vietnamese youth.

As we reflect on the implications of corporal punishment and its impact on children's lives, we are reminded of our collective responsibility to safeguard their rights and well-being. It is imperative that we stand united in our commitment to ending the cycle of violence and advocating for policies that prioritize children's safety, dignity, and future. Together, we have the power to create a society where every child in Vietnam (including us!!) can thrive free from the fear of violence and abuse.
