Are we all getting a fair shot at education in Vietnam?

Abstract: The right to equal access to education for children is one of the fundamental rights recognized and protected by both international and national law. This right ensures that all children, regardless of race, gender, age, ethnicity, disability, or economic background, are given the same opportunity to access education.

Although this is a basic right guaranteed by international and national laws, in practice, there remain significant disparities in access to education among different groups of children. Many countries, including Vietnam, continue to face challenges in daily life that not only hinder school attendance but also affect the quality of education.

This journal aims to provide a comprehensive, multidimensional perspective on children's right to equal access to education and propose several measures to promote this right for disadvantaged groups.

Introduction

Education provides not only knowledge but also life skills, critical thinking, and societal adaptability. While equal access to education is a fundamental human right, many children globally still lack this opportunity. Factors such as the need to work to support families, gender biases preventing girls from attending school, and economic barriers hinder access, especially in developing countries.

Disadvantaged children—such as those with disabilities, ethnic minorities, and abandoned children—face even greater challenges in obtaining equal educational opportunities. Ensuring equal access to education across various groups remains a significant global challenge.

According to the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights, equal access to education is essential not only for acquiring knowledge but also for empowerment and the realization of other human rights. Promoting equal access allows girls to close the gender gap, enables poor children to escape poverty, and facilitates the integration of children with disabilities into society. Thus, the right to education is a fundamental human right and a vital tool for achieving the fulfillment of other rights.

Theoretical framework

According to Article 1 of the 1989 UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), a child is defined as anyone under 18, unless legal adulthood is reached earlier. Children are vulnerable due to lower social status, cognitive development, and inability to self-protect. Within this group, subsets like disabled, ethnic minority, migrant, and refugee children face even greater challenges, particularly in accessing education.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) asserts: "Everyone has the right to education." Children are no exception. Furthermore, Article 28, Section 1, of the 1990 UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) states: "States Parties recognize the right of the child to education, and with a view to achieving this right progressively and on the basis of equal opportunity..." This emphasizes that the right to education applies to all children without any discrimination against beneficiaries of this right.

The provisions require states to ensure equal access to education for all children. UNESCO’s 1960 Convention Against Discrimination in Education defines this as education free from discrimination based on race, gender, language, religion, or social origin. This implies that all children, regardless of social diversity or physical challenges, have the right to equal educational opportunities.

Most discussions about equality in education focus on how to equalize access and participation in various levels of formal education for different social groups (Lynch, 2000) [1]. According to Froomkin, there is a need for equality in access to education for children from different socio-economic backgrounds. However, while the discussion of equal access to education tends to center on equal opportunity, in reality, this definition only reflects one aspect of the concept of equal access to education. Campbell and Klein (1982) argue that focusing solely on equal opportunity overlooks the broader concept of lifelong educational access, which starts at birth and continues throughout life.

Kathleen Lynch approaches "equality" from the perspective of "equality of condition." Simply put, the definition of equality of condition is the belief that people should be as equal as possible in terms of the central conditions of their lives. Equality of condition is not about making equality more just or providing more equal opportunities; rather, it ensures that people have almost equal prospects for a good life. In this sense, equal access to education means that all children have equal opportunities to access and enjoy this right.

"Equality" is often mistaken for "sameness," but they are different concepts, especially in education. Equality means everyone has the same opportunities, not that everyone should be treated or taught the same way. All children, regardless of race, gender, religion, disability, or economic background, deserve equal access to quality education. However, achieving this requires tailored support based on individual needs. For instance, a child with a disability might need assistive devices, a low-income child may need financial help, and an ethnic minority child might require language assistance.

"Equality" more closely aligns with "equity," as each child begins from different circumstances and experiences varying impacts on their rights. Inequalities in birth circumstances—such as country of birth, family education status, socio-economic level, cultural environment, age of education, school resources, and teacher competency—affect opportunities for equal education. For example, a child born in a Brazilian slum lacks the same educational access as a child born in Paris.

When natural inequalities arise from how individuals are born, countries and legal bodies strive to make those who are not equal in nature legally equal. When natural inequalities combine with social inequalities, as Rousseau (1950) mentioned in his Social Contract Theory, it is impossible to speak of equal educational opportunities for students. Consequently, creating "equality" in access to education requires creating "equitable" opportunities through responsible entities such as the state, families, schools, and social organizations, which must respect the individual differences and specific needs of child groups while supporting efforts to bridge the educational gap.

Legal framework

As an active member of the Convention on the Rights of the Child, Vietnam prioritizes "education and training as the foremost national policy," enacting numerous policies related to children's right to education. The 1946 Constitution established that "children have the right to care and education," with primary education declared compulsory and free, particularly for poor students. These principles have been reaffirmed in subsequent Constitutions, culminating in Article 39 of the 2013 Constitution, which states, "Citizens have the right and obligation to learn."

Vietnamese law has institutionalized children's rights through legal frameworks such as the 2013 Constitution, the 2016 Child Law, and the 2019 Education Law, focusing on comprehensive child development. Article 16 of the 2016 Child Law emphasizes that children have the right to education, equal opportunities for learning, and the development of their talents and creativity. Promoting access to education, particularly for marginalized groups in challenging socio-economic conditions, is a key objective of Vietnam's development policies.

The 2019 Education Law reinforces that "learning is the right and obligation of citizens," ensuring equal educational opportunities for all, regardless of ethnicity, religion, gender, or socio-economic status. Article 13 emphasizes the state's responsibility to ensure fairness in education and provide a quality learning environment, particularly for vulnerable children, including those with disabilities and those from low-income families.

To support equal rights for children with disabilities, Vietnam ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) and enacted the 2010 Law on Persons with Disabilities, which guarantees full educational opportunities. Vietnamese law prohibits actions that hinder children's education, establishing responsibilities for families, schools, and state agencies. Article 69 of the 2014 Law on Marriage and Family outlines parental obligations regarding children's education, while Article 99 of the 2016 Child Law mandates that parents, teachers, and caregivers ensure children's rights to learn and complete compulsory education.

Decree 81/2021/ND-CP details tuition fee exemptions and reductions for various groups, including children from families with historical merits, those without caregivers, and students from poor households. The Decree also allows for 50-70% tuition fee reductions and provisions for suspending fees in cases of natural disasters or epidemics, subject to local government decisions.

Key achievements and areas for continued development

4.1. Key Achievements

In recent years, Vietnam has made impressive strides in promoting equal access to education for children, contributing to improved education quality and ensuring the comprehensive development of future generations. These achievements are evident not only through policies and legal documents but also via specific programs and projects aimed at improving learning conditions for children across different regions.

One of the most remarkable accomplishments is achieving universal primary education, with a 100% enrollment rate from grades 1 to 5. Fee exemptions and reductions for students from poor and near-poor households, as well as other vulnerable groups, have enabled many disadvantaged children to continue their education without interruption. Notably, policies supporting educational costs, free textbooks, and social assistance have alleviated the financial burden on poor families, ensuring equal access to education for children.

Additionally, Vietnam has launched several educational development programs targeting remote, mountainous, and ethnic minority regions. Initiatives to construct and upgrade schools, along with modern facilities and equipment, have substantially improved teaching and learning conditions, thereby enhancing the overall quality of education in these regions. By 2020, the literacy rate among ethnic minorities reached 90%; the percentage of solidly constructed schools increased from 77.1% in 2015 to 91.3% in 2019, and the school attendance rate across all educational levels improved... [2]

Ensuring the right to education for children with disabilities has received special attention in Vietnam. The country has enhanced access to education for disabled children by establishing provincial Centers for Inclusive Education Support in over 20 provinces and cities, as well as 107 specialized educational institutions and 12 centers dedicated to these children. Data from the National Survey on People with Disabilities shows that the primary school attendance rate for children with disabilities is approximately 88.7%, while the rate for high school is 33.6% [3].

For girls, Vietnam has also undertaken measures to promote gender education and protect their rights. Gender education programs in schools and awareness campaigns on girls' rights have helped reduce early school dropout rates, child marriages, and gender-based violence. This not only improves the educational outcomes of girls but also raises community awareness about the importance of gender equality in education.

4.2. Areas that needs improvements

Despite the achievements, there remain issues that need further research and adjustment to align with practical needs:

First, there are existing policies that prioritize enrollment and support for preschool, elementary, and high school students from ethnic minority groups (Decree 57/2017/ND-CP), focusing on children from particularly disadvantaged areas (Region III). However, more support is needed for children in Regions I and II, where many ethnic minority families face economic difficulties, affecting children's education. Additionally, the changes in the curriculum and textbooks, along with teacher quality, have posed challenges.

Second, most education and vocational training policies for ethnic minority students are currently gender-neutral. To avoid gender biases, specific regulations are needed to promote equal access to education for girls. Although the Education Law (2019) and the Vocational Education Law (2014) include measures to close the gap in access to general and vocational education for ethnic minorities, most of these provisions are "gender-neutral." The Gender Equality Law (2006) ensures gender equality in education, but greater attention is needed to address gender-specific issues within ethnic minority communities.

Third, policies on inclusive and culturally diverse education need to be further researched and adjusted, particularly in educational programs and textbooks. Language barriers remain a significant obstacle for many ethnic minority children in accessing education. These students follow a standardized curriculum, with the primary language of instruction being Vietnamese and an elective foreign language, often English. Therefore, further research is required to develop solutions that facilitate inclusive education for these children.

Fourth, in addition to issuing legal documents on children's right to equal access to education, law enforcement and practical application are crucial. Therefore, assessing the quality of law enforcement in different regions and for specific groups of children is essential. In rural, mountainous, and remote areas, the implementation of educational support policies continues to face many challenges due to a lack of resources and infrastructure, leading to disparities in educational access compared to urban areas.

Recommendations

Based on the strengths and limitations mentioned above, I propose several recommendations to further promote equal access to education for children:

1. Legal policies should focus more on children with special circumstances. It is essential to clearly identify and categorize different groups of children, understanding their specific needs to formulate and implement appropriate measures that promote equal access to education. The ultimate goal is to ensure that every child enjoys the right to equal access to education.

2. Legal regulations need to be implemented swiftly and uniformly, avoiding overlap or complexity that may hinder their application. Legal provisions should focus on children's right to education, avoiding the perception of children as passive recipients and instead acknowledging the rights children can exercise themselves. Furthermore, regulations must be specific and avoid being overly general, which could reduce the effectiveness of legal dissemination and education efforts.

3. To ensure equal access to education for ethnic minority children in remote areas and vocational education for persons with disabilities, it is crucial to continue strengthening policies for teachers and educational managers working in special schools and regions with difficult socio-economic conditions. Special attention should also be given to teaching and learning the languages and scripts of ethnic minorities within educational institutions.

4. Continue establishing policies and implementing reforms to ensure the quality of education for all. These policies should set standards, frameworks, and guidelines for curricula, assessment, and teacher qualifications. Education authorities must study and consider the viewpoints of various stakeholders, such as educators, parents, and experts in the education sector, in an effort to create policies that align with the evolving needs of society.

5. Legal policies should be adjusted to expand the beneficiaries of the right to access education. For example, simplifying administrative procedures is necessary. Education policies should reduce complex bureaucratic processes, making it easier for children from disadvantaged backgrounds to enroll in schools. Education laws need to be improved to ensure that all children have equal opportunities to access education, contributing to the sustainable development of society.

Conclusion

The significance of education for individuals and society is clear, and the right to education cannot be subject to market principles. Equal access must be guaranteed for all children, including those from impoverished or special circumstances.

Economic and political obstacles often prevent young people from receiving a full education. Structural issues like poverty, discrimination, and conflict hinder school attendance and access to learning materials. Therefore, responsible parties must implement measures to promote equal access to education for all children.
