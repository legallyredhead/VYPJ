Vietnam’s Culture and Its Effects in Advancing Vietnam’s Cause for Independence

Vietnam, through its 5000 years of governmental construction and war for independence (Vietnamese National Police Force, 2019), has acquired an extremely intricate and unique culture. The identity of the Vietnamese, though being subjected to the challenges of multiple eras of colonization and occupation, was and still is maintained, staying true to Ho Chi Minh’s teachings of integration and adoption of good qualities but not assimilation (translation of the terms “hòa nhập nhưng không hòa tan”). Moreover, not only are these cultural traits sustained, but they also play pivotal roles in aiding the Vietnamese cause for independence and advancement on the world stage.

The Vietnamese language is the first example of such use of culture in Vietnam’s cause for independence. It is important to note that Vietnam has 2 alphabet systems in history. The first one, called “Nôm,” is a Chinese alphabet-inspired language invented in the Vietnamese Confucianism era, with its first purpose being to demonstrate Vietnamese-specific vocabulary in written forms. The language was allowed to take shape for a while, however, during the invasion of Vietnam by the Ming dynasty, almost all documentations of such language were burned, halting the development of the language. However, after gaining independence, the Nôm language was no longer suppressed, allowing more teaching and usage of it. Gradually, many poems and writings were published in the Nôm language, establishing a rich literature culture in the country. Despite such development, the Nôm language was unfortunately wiped out during the French colonial era, replaced by the “national alphabet system” (“chữ quốc ngữ”) which followed the Latin style of writing. This change, in no way, demonstrated Vietnam’s loss in the cultural battle against the French; in fact, quite the opposite is true. Despite the language being the result of the French’s efforts to erase the Vietnamese Confucianism route, the new Romanized alphabet turned out to be much simpler to write and thus, much easier to be taught and spread. Though the credit for such widespread understanding of the language remains debated, it is undeniable that the first few newspapers written in the language played great roles in it despite their Western-leaning content. The educated population of Vietnam at the time, after enduring the harsh conditions of the French government, was fueled with a motive to resist and fight for independence, and with the popularization of the language, a more risky but effective move was prompted - the establishment of a revolutionary newspaper. In fact, like many other revolutions, the Vietnamese revolution started with simply that, revolutionary newspapers written in French and Vietnamese led by Ho Chi Minh, who later became the leader of the Vietnamese uprising against the French colonial government and further after, the Vietnamese Communist Party leader. It is famously said by Ho Chi Minh himself that the pen is a powerful weapon, and he was not wrong. The sustenance of the Vietnamese language had served good for the revolutionary cause, keeping Vietnamese citizens away from the risk of assimilation by Western forces, and later, fueling a successful revolution. With that, the Vietnamese Declaration of Independence was written and read in the national language by Ho Chi Minh in 1945, marking Vietnam’s success in gaining independence as a country with its own language. Afterward, during Vietnam’s fight for reunification, political writings and posters in the language promoting the Ho Chi Minh government continued to aid the process and strengthen nationalism in the Vietnamese people’s hearts.

Secondly, the Vietnamese’s Red music and literature should also be mentioned as extremely effective political tools in Vietnam. During the era, the most highlighted traits in both of these platforms were the collective strength and nationalism of the Vietnamese people, as they courageously surpassed challenges with the hope of acquiring independence and, ultimately, widespread happiness for their nation. However, it would be completely misjudged to think that these contemporary pieces lack variety. In fact, such a trait of the Vietnamese people was demonstrated using different images and settings. For literature, many poems implemented the frequent use of natural components to illustrate the Vietnamese force, such as the bamboo tree, as used in pieces like “Cây tre Việt Nam” (translated as “The Vietnamese Bamboo Tree”). Other writings featured soul-touching camaraderie between Vietnamese soldiers, as seen in “Dồng chí” (or “Comrades”). Another kind of red literature is short stories, such as “Chiếc lược ngà” or “Lặng lẽ Sa Pa” (roughly translated as “The ivory comb” and “Silent Sa Pa”), which followed the lives of Vietnamese revolutionary forces. These stories served to further advance the revolutionary cause and encourage all Vietnamese citizens to partake in the fight for freedom and independence. Vietnamese red music, though less frequently talked about, is just as special to the cause. These songs, like red writings, featured the many stories of Vietnamese revolutionary personnel, documenting the sorrows of seeing their hometown demolished or sadness amidst the lack of food, fueling more determination to fight for independence and reunification. Moreover, many of these songs also carry positive messages, such as hope for independence and trust in the government, as well as heart-wrenching love declarations amidst the raging war. These forms of art and culture accompanied Vietnam through the early independence era as well, as Vietnam emerged as a new and struggling country, barely recovering from the sorrows and weight of the fighting it had just endured. Literature and music in this era captured not only the strangeness of the new era, having come back from the military base such as in “Ánh trăng” (“Moonlight”) and the struggle of the new era, but also hope for the development of the country as well as for multiple different industries to prosper.

In the modern days, these key cultural pieces, though not as popular as the modern pop sound or the new echoes of literature, are still thoroughly preserved. The Vietnamese language is spoken by almost all Vietnamese citizens and is the official language of Vietnam. The language even spread worldwide, inspiring many foreigners to pick up Vietnamese. Revolutionary literature is taught at schools and is included in the compulsory curriculum. Music of the revolutionary era can still be heard through the voices of both senior and aspiring artists, allowing the youth of Vietnam to understand the culture and the power of Vietnamese arts in wartime.
