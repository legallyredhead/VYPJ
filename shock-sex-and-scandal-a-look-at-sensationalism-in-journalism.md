Shock, Sex, and Scandal: A look at Sensationalism in Journalism and Media of Vietnam

“Journalists and reporters who want to write well must, first and foremost, write correctly.”

- Định Nam, Người làm báo

Introduction

The media landscape of Vietnam, in general, has always been one of great complexity and controversy. Journalism and media in this nation are woven with deep-rooted social, economical, and political issues that are challenging to both navigate and comprehend. Within this environment, one of the most prominent concerns that, unfortunately, is still on the rise, is the influence of sensationalism.

Sensationalism, characterized by its penchant for exaggerated or emotionally charged content, has unfortunately entrenched itself within mass media and news outlets in Vietnam. With its roots from tabloid journalism (Wang, 2012), sensationalism prevails - from the purposive selection of emotive language in headlines to the allure of infotainment, where entertainment supersedes information of social significance and value, the act is omnipotent in Vietnam’s mass media.

Adopting an issue-commentary standpoint, this research endeavors to dissect the underlying mechanisms driving sensationalism, identify the impacts this phenomenon has on Vietnamese youth, and offer solutions and policy recommendations aimed at mitigating the =issue at hand. Even though sensationalism also consists of the purposive use of visual stimuli, this paper will only take into account the textual elements of the news themselves, including the title and the news written, and eschewing visual components. By focusing solely on textual elements and using real-world examples from Vietnamese news outlets to support its arguments, this study aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the issue at hand.

All in all, this paper aims to shed light on sensationalism - a critical aspect of Vietnam's media landscape, in hope of laying the groundwork for informed discourse and actionable change in the future for Vietnamese youth.

Issue overview and analysis

Sensationalism and infotainment - a two-sided relationship

It is not difficult to find low-quality and over-sensationalized news on the digital news outlets in Vietnam. Sensationalism, as defined by Otto in his paper “The Softening of Journalistic Political Communication”, in which he presents a multilevel framework model of softening of political communication, is “Coverage with making use of journalistic strategies and verbal/visual production features, aiming at assumed effects on audience.” (Otto, 2016) It is also in this paper that Otto argues it is important to take into consideration both sides of sensationalism - that is, the production side (content, visual style…) and the ‘effect’ side from readers. “Sensationalism represents a specific kind of journalistic coverage aiming at triggering certain recipient reactions (attention, emotions) by using specific sensationalist production features within a single news item.” Thus, in order for sensationalism to be effective, both the audience and the journalist have to play a role: news outlets producing and the audience engaging. Therefore, simply put, sensationalism is the intentional production of news coverage that aims to evoke desired reactions from recipients, which is the average reader or news consumer.

The main issues of sensationalism in Vietnam media coverage that this paper has identified are over-exaggerated news titles, unverified images and information, and a lack of substantial or valuable news. The latter is especially prominent in ‘infotainment’ - a combination of information and entertainment, a neologism used to refer to the hybrid style of journalism that, according to the Cambridge dictionary, is “the reporting of news and facts in an entertaining and humorous way rather than providing real information”. Smaller digital press agencies are usually guilty of this, however, it is important to note that established news outlets in Vietnam also employ both sensationalist news and infotainment as well; examples of which will be analyzed later in the paper. Infotainment, by its nature, is not harmful - it is simply a tendency of news outlets to produce news that are more ‘celebrity centered’, and Wang divides the definition of ‘Celebrity news” into four subcategories: “, sports, culture (entertainment industry), royalty, and celebrities (celebrities of great notoriety, less famous celebrities, and other) (Wang, 2012).

Competition and economic pressure

The problem lies in the tabloid journalism approach that can be seen increasing in Vietnam media due to its extreme competition and growing demand for information by citizens: As of 2023, there was a total of 671 magazines and 127 press agencies; with 41,000 active members of the journalism industry (Infographics, 2023). Fueled by a views-driven motive and to keep their audiences as engaged as possible during this era of mass information, news outlets find themselves pressured to deliver news that tend to the mass’ need for entertainment in such a fast pace that quality is often neglected and entertainment prioritized. In other words, since “[journalists and news managers] are forced to borrow and adapt characteristics from entertainment genres and modes of conversation and communication that privilege an informal style with its emphasis on storytelling skills and spectacle” (Thussu, 2015), this results in a tabloid approach and low-quality infotainment and sensationalized news on the media landscape of Vietnam.

The overt use of sensationalism is fueled by economic imperatives within the media industry, where agencies resort to sensationalist tactics in a bid to drive up viewership amidst fierce competition and economical changes. “Vietnamese media often attempt to gain profits by publishing sensational content while at the same time abiding by the Communist Party’s interests.” (Yen-Khanh, 2022). This in effect creates a hybrid media environment of Vietnam; a combination of neoliberalism and authoritarianism, of which breeds a commercialized and profit driven industry of mass media.

Another crucial component that adds to the extreme competition in the news industry is the popularity of social media. According to the statistics by the Ministry of Information and Communications, in 20221, access to digital news systems had decreased by more than 10%, even though there are an astounding 77 million active users of social media in Vietnam as of 2023, which is roughly 71% of the population. With instant and free access to information, it can be observed that the mass now have a tendency to gravitate short and condensed, and usually unverified, information on social media platforms.

Shock, Sex, and Scandal

Researcher Nguyễn Đình Hậu at the University of Social Sciences and Humanities coined the phrase "Shock, Sex, and 'Sến" (direct translation: cheesy) to describe the tendency of presses to resort to sensational topics that often revolve around shocking news, sexual content, and 'Sến’ - meaning the informal language used in such reports, under economic pressures and competition. This paper proposes that the latter be replaced by another component: Scandal.

From a linguistic perspective, the media landscape of Vietnam is rife with deliberate use of overly sensitive and strong adjectives to evoke curiosity among readers - a phenomenon that can be viewed as one of the consequences of a views-driven industry.

According to a study presented in Bao Nghe An (Nghe An News), readers tend to gravitate toward news that contain clickbaiting headlines; in the following order:

Titles that contain exclamatory words

Long titles

Titles that contain adverbs, verbs, adjectives

Short titles

Contain questions the audience or use words that suggest questioning in readers

Adding to this sentiment, Digital Journalism Professor Vũ Cường of Academy of Journalism and Communication argues that though the importance of crafting an engaging title is undeniable in the press industry, many journalists are violating professional ethics by creating sensational titles for topics that are “immoral or of social values”. Though the study by Slattery et al. (2001) argues that topics that are inherently sensational, for instance, crimes and accidents, will inevitably sensationalize the news about them, it is the practice of intentional and at times, immoral title-crafting by journalists in Vietnam that begs the question of unethical practice of sensationalism. In addition, the problem does not solely lie in these titles; it is the presentation of the news itself that is extremely sensationalized and of low-quality. “ [The publications] purvey the weird and the salacious in crime stories culled from police records, all published in the name of exposing social problems.” (Heng, 2000). Below this paper will provide examples and analysis for the three categories of shock, sex, and scandal.

1c. SHOCK and SCANDAL

Upon clicking on the tag “Shocking news” (Translation: Tin sốc) of any type of Vietnamese digital newspapers or magazines, one can find a myriad of sensationalized news that offer little value or knowledge to readers, especially news that focus on celebrities. This type of shock-bait is much more common with ‘infotainment’ – the news of celebrities and the ‘showbiz’, or accidents that involve deaths or casualties. These types of news are identifiable in the click-baiting titles, with popular words including “sốc”, “kinh hoàng”, “choáng với” (Tú, 2015). These overly expressive and emotional words suggest a lack of objectivity of the news, which can create a tendency for readers to undermine such news. One concern that arises from these constant use of adjectives is desensitization of readers from emotional news. Administrator of The Forum, Mr. Mai Phan Lợi, proposes a legitimate hypothetical situation: Two traffic accidents, one resulted in 6 deaths, the other 18. Both are reported with the title: Horrifying: traffic accident resulted in 6/18 deaths. This will create confusion for the reader, as they are unable to grasp what qualifies as being ‘horrifying’, and how many deaths can be qualified as such.

In some cases, the titles do not contain any such adjectives, but are completely misleading and, instead of being a title, it acts as a statement, which parades as legitimate information and can mislead readers if they only skim through the titles - a habit that many have. An absurd example is from 2013, when multiple credible news sites ran titles that concern the health conditions of Superstar Angelina Jolie. “Superstar Angelina Jolie can only live for around 3 more years” was the title of an article on Thanh Niên, a recognizable press with a long history in Vietnam. The declarative tone of the title can actually mislead readers into believing that the information is confirmed and accurate. However, upon clicking on the article, it offers no explanation on why the celebrity could only live for 3 more years, but rather only details irrelevant information about Jolie’s life after her breast surgery. The article shows no evidence or citation that can back up its very much exaggerated and outright misleading claim. In addition, when searching for this title, the average reader will find the nearly exact article posted on multiple other outlets, consequently mistaking the news to be true. To put it shortly, this is borderline misinformation.

2c. SEX

Even credential and established news outlets in Vietnam can also sometimes be guilty of tabloid journalism, especially when it comes to the topic of female celebrities. The misogyny are sometimes deep-rooted within the reporting of such topics, and are not hard to identify. These female celebrities are usually painted in a sexual light, with articles that sometimes neglect totally their talent and only focus on their appearance or sexual appeal. In these cases, the average reader can find words meant to pique interest and arousal with high frequency of appearance, including but not limited to: Leaked (lộ/lộ hàng/lộ ảnh), “showing off one’s figure” (khoe/thả dáng) (Hậu, 2015). Another few that this paper has identified are “bỏng mắt với" (eye-burning), “fiery/on fire" (bốc lửa), and “provocative" (gợi cảm).

Take the article “Loạt ảnh thon gọn sexy của Mỹ Tâm đập tan tin đồn mang thai 4 tháng với "phi công trẻ” (translation: “My Tam’s sexy figure in recent photos abolished pregnancy allegations with her young lover”), published in 2020 by Dân Việt, for instance. Though the article did briefly mention her talent, the rest of the paper are slideshows of pictures of the artist My Tam in revealing clothes, with little to no captions under each. The article also shows a hint of fatphobia - mentioning how My Tam once accidentally showed her “thick thighs and unflattering waist”, as well as constant praises for the singer’s ‘successful’ weight loss transformation: “The singer has successfully lost weight, hence she can confidently wear tight clothes.” The meaning and message of this article is questionable, non-existent even. It plays two roles: One as a sexually-charged dose of content, and another as insecurity and beauty standards consolidation and reminder for other Vietnamese women. And of course, the news is not substantial in any way, is sexual in nature, and devoid of meaning and knowledge.

In summary, shock, sex, and scandal are the three most common shortcomings of Vietnam’s production of tabloid journalism and media.

III. Impact on youth

There has been little to no research done in regards to the impact of sensational news and titles on the Vietnamese audience. However, various foreign studies have pointed out how the intentional reporting of news, or generally speaking, sensationalism in different media can have an effect on the audience. This paper identifies two main issues arising from sensationalism that can have a considerable impact on Vietnamese youth: Desensitization and Perceived Language Intensity.

Desensitization

Repeated exposure to sensationalized news can have desensitizing effects on the audience. (Lowe, 2016) uses prime examples from the past, including 'shocking' events, including the Vietnam War and the First Moon Landing and studies how the press coverage presents such news, and the effects it had on the audience. Through the analyses, it is concluded that the growth of mass production of news also entails the growth of sensationalism, and this, in effect, desensitizes the audience. "[...] as audiences were exposed to a greater amount of sensational content, they became desensitized to it, which in turn raised the level of shock value a piece of work must possess in order to elicit a response from the audience." This constant cycle of repeated exposure of shocking news produced by the press and desensitization of the audience only exacerbate the tendency of presses to resort to sensationalism further as an attempt to draw attention and evoke responses from the audience, hence gaining more profit.

There have been various studies that suggest a correlation between constant exposure to sensationalized news, especially violent news, and desensitization. (Anderson, 2003) findings reveal that media violence increases aggression and violent behaviors; short-term exposure leads to the "likelihood of physically and verbally aggressive behavior, aggressive thoughts, and aggressive emotions", while long-term exposure may result in behavioral aggression later in life, including domestic abuse and assaults. While not concerning the press media but rather video games, a study in 2009 also supports the hypothesis exposure to violent media will decrease helping behavior in people and "make people numb to the pain and sufferings of others." (Bushman, 2009)

2. Perceived Language Intensity

One of the effects that sensationalism can have on the audience is manipulating and evoking readers' emotions. Language intensity is a concept whose definition is rather unclear and "problematic" (Liebrecht, 2019). In its simplest form, it refers to the intensity of language created by the use of language intensifiers, also known as adverbs. (Renkema, 1997) proposes that language intensity can take the following forms: word replacement (horrible instead of bad), word addition (awfully bad), rhetorical devices (e.g. metaphors), or typographical elements (cool!!! instead of cool).

Seeing how language intensifiers can influence the audience, (Burgers & de Graaf, 2013) proposes that language intensity has been employed as a feature of sensationalism in print media. Using Perceived Language Intensity (PLI) as the metric, the researchers carried out two experiments to test their hypotheses concerning how the use of intensifiers and framing of news can a) increase PLI b) have an affect on "newsworthiness, attitude towards the news article and (c) belief content" and c) how positive/negative framing of content using intensifiers can increase PLI. Overall, the two studies' results mainly confirmed that language intensifiers have in effect on PLI, "indicating that readers indeed experienced texts with intensifiers as more intense and arousing". One of the experiments also suggests that the use of intensifiers may decrease the quality and credibility of the news.

The issue of manipulating the audience through language intensity is that since the majority of sensationalized news is negative, it can also encourage harmful behaviors in readers as well. An article in the ​​Al Jazeera Media Institute gives suggestions on how suicides should be reported ethically, or else it may lead to 'copycat' effects of people imitating the tragic act that they see on the news. "Including specifics about the method of suicide should be avoided in coverage, as it could potentially serve as a model for others. Furthermore, details concerning the individual's personal life, including images of the suicide location, any notes left behind, or other personal information, should also be omitted." (Ayyoub, 2023). However, when reporting cases of suicides, many presses in Vietnam are still guilty of including images, or even videos, of the suicide (or attempts) in the articles. For instance, an article in Thanh Niên: "Bảo vệ trường kể giây phút cứu nữ sinh lớp 6 treo lơ lửng trên tầng cao" (Rough translation: School's guard recounts the moment he saved 6th-grade school girl dangling up high) in 2021 includes a 1 minute and 30 seconds video of the girl holding on to the rail of the balcony, with voiceovers reporting the news in the background. This overt use of the medium as a sensational feature undoubtedly would not only cause uneasiness in readers but can also act as a model for many other naive and mentally unstable youngsters.

3. Narratives and attitudes shaping

Unfortunately various tabloid reports of scandal or celebrity news, especially female celebrities, reek of misogyny. This might be a result of a deep-rooted patriarchal society that Vietnam developed from, paired with the rigid beauty standards that have been exacerbated by social media and editing apps. News of female celebrities are usually presented in a much sexual light, as well as unnecessary emphasis or commentary on their appearance. The way the press refers to women is also jarring: they are usually defined by their marriage with their (usually more popular) husband, or more surprisingly, their pregnancy or parental status. One of the most popular phrases are “gái một con", usually followed by a compliment of the celebrities’ body. IV. Conclusion and Suggestions

It might be intuitively suggested to the average reader that in order to minimize sensationalism, press agencies should be controlled more strictly. However, I would argue against this sentiment, as it goes against the free, uncensored spirit of journalism in general and of reporting and crafting of news by journalists. What is needed are not rules - but rather, guidelines and policies for journalists to use as reference. The truth remains that sensationalism will continue to exist as long as there is an audience and its need for provocative, shocking, and scandalous news. It is unavoidable - the tendency for sensational news is and will continue to be the byproduct of this age of mass information where each individual is bombarded with constant stimuli. There are, however, methods to alleviate the phenomenon. Firstly, there should be clear and unambiguous policies and professional ethical codes for journalisms. This would help journalists to navigate the complexities of sensationalism and be aware of ethical reporting practices. Secondly, there should be training and education for ethical reporting for journalists. There can be government-funded training sessions and workshops for journalists to educate them on the topic of ethical reporting and presenting of news. And finally, proper and ethical news outlets that have not ‘degenerated' quality-wise and given in to the trend of sensationalism (which is scarce) should be promoted and recommended to readers in Vietnam.

Summary (for PR only)

“Journalists and reporters who want to write well must, first and foremost, write correctly.”

- Định Nam, Người làm báo

The principles of journalism - reporting true and meaningful information have stood for decades and serve as the foundation of Vietnamese journalism. However, as publishing firms and journalists nowadays are met with an enhanced level of economic stress and competition, sensationalism, characterized by its penchant for exaggerated or emotionally charged content, has unfortunately entrenched itself within mass media and news outlets in Vietnam. This led to multiple adverse effects on journalism itself, such as the using of clickbaits and the reporting of “shock, sex, and scandal” news to attract readers. Moreover, sensationalism leave lasting effects on the youth, as they become desensitized or misguided. Though these effects are undesirable, this report believes control over press agencies is not the answer, but rather a mixture of guidelines and education should be implemented in the press industry.
