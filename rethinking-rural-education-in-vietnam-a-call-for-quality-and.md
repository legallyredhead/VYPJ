Rethinking Rural Education in Vietnam: A Call for Quality and Relevance

This piece aims to question the supposed impacts of rural education investment prioritization strategies and thus argues for new quality-oriented education policies for Vietnam rural areas that prioritize students at the foremost.

Note that my primary stance here is that existing forms of investment priorities like infrastructures investment, enrollment rates, returns to schools are crucial and remain very much so deserving of education investment budgets. However, I believe that the shift now needs to be towards improving the quality and the tangible gains a student from these areas can receive from education, rather than bigger schools or more students when the education quality itself in these areas suffer immense deficiencies.

Introduction

The widely celebrated narrative of rural students relocating to cities, securing university degrees from prestigious urban universities, and landing lucrative jobs, as rural students best outcomes from further education, represents an elitist and urban-centric approach. This narrative in education policies not only marginalizes those unable to achieve this ideal due to various constraints—be it capacity, resources, or circumstances—but also overlooks the realities and the need for quality education of millions of rural students.

My motivation to address this issue stems from observing how education for rural students is idealized and promoted by governmental advocacy as a vehicle for urban-centric social mobility through university education and urban relocation. This perspective is inherently elitist and saturated with urban bias, given that nearly 70% of our population resides in non-urban areas. Such a narrow view of education's role translates into policies and objectives for rural regions that adopt a one-size-fits-all approach, suitable only for urban contexts. The skills necessary for rural development and contribution are markedly different, demanding an education system that fosters skills relevant to rural economies, such as agriculture and local industries, beyond just academic knowledge aimed at urban integration. Expecting rural students to invest years in an education system fantasizing about improbable outcomes of urban social mobility is fundamentally unjust. Education should empower individuals with practical skills for tangible benefits, not lead them on a futile chase for improbable outcomes. The harsh truth is that despite the push for high school completion under the guise of meeting top-down government targets, the quality of education often falls short of enabling rural students to access prestigious urban universities.

Consequently, many find themselves in the same position as their predecessors: engaged in agriculture or factory work with no significant returns on their years of educational pursuits. We must advocate for education policies that genuinely serve rural students, providing them with relevant skills and knowledge that enhance their lives and contribute to their communities, rather than perpetuating a cycle of unfulfilled aspirations and wasted resources.

The challenge, then, is to reimagine an educational framework that values and cultivates the rich potential of rural areas. This involves moving beyond traditional knowledge acquisition to embrace a curriculum that prepares students for tangible achievements in their communities. By doing so, education can become a cornerstone of rural development, tailored to the specific needs of these areas and aligned with the broader goals of economic restructuring and rural revitalization.

The current state of rural education in Vietnam

This brings me to dissect the current issues, challenges identified with Vietnam rural education policies, and to give more specific analysis with the examples that I just bring forth. These challenges will primarily deal with the scope of quality of rural education.

EXISTING POLICIES

If general education is viewed under its four dimensions, i.e., access, quality, efficiency, and equity, then it can be said Vietnam has made significant strides in access development, but remained weak in improving quality, efficiency, and equity. These shortcomings persist chiefly and significantly in rural education. The country now has a very high literacy rate; in 2015, 94.5 percent of the Vietnamese population aged 15 and over could read and write (Central Intelligence Agency, 2019). The country also has the highest level of public spending on education of any other ASEAN countries (Rynhart & Chang, 2014). Previous studies show that there was an increasing rate of return to education from 1993 to 2008 (Doan & Gibson, 2012; Gallup, 2004; Liu, 2004; Pham & Reilly, 2007).

The current education administrators are well-aware of the needed improvements and the approach towards better quality, though the primary focus has always been access to education.

The most recent phase of education reform in Vietnam has centered around the 2011–2020 Education Development Strategic Plan. The strategic plan aimed to improve access to and the quality of all levels of education, including preschool, general education, vocational education, and continuing education. Eight key solutions were proposed to address the main challenges identified: (1) renovating education management and administration; (2) promoting teachers and education managers’ development; (3) renovating content and teaching methods, examination, testing, and education assessment; (4) increasing investment of resources and renewing education finance distribution mechanisms; (5) strengthening the link between universities and the labor market as well as scientific research and technology transfer to meet societal needs; (6) fostering educational development for disadvantaged areas, ethnic minorities, and social policy beneficiaries; (7) promoting education science; and (8) expanding and improving the effectiveness of international cooperation in education.

Many past progress has been made towards quality teaching, especially the adoption of Dakar Education for All (EFA) Framework for Action – an initiative with greater focus on quality than those set out in the Millennium Development Goals for the 2015 curriculum reforms initiatives. The key transformations of this new curriculum was the approval of a new competency-based curriculum that has been implemented ever since the beginning of 2020. This curriculum renovation is following a competency-based approach, according to which the post-2015 curriculum of general education will aim at nurturing and developing the generic core competencies for students in school and in life, including: (a) competencies for self-development (self-learning, problem-solving, critical thinking, creativity and innovation, and self-management); (b) competencies in social relations (communication and cooperation); and (c) competencies in using tools interactively (language, media, information, communications, and technology).

Despite the government's dedication to enhancing education quality through competency-based teaching methods and curriculum reforms, there is a stark need for clearer guidelines and support to actualize these competency-based approaches, particularly in rural areas. Here, the implementation of these well-intentioned policies often ends up being incomplete, caught in a limbo that is 'neither fish nor fowl'. This ambiguity significantly hampers the effectiveness of educational reforms in rural settings, where the gap between policy and practice becomes most evident, posing substantial challenges in delivering genuine value to students.

In recent years, concerns have surfaced regarding the efficacy and direction of Vietnam's education investments, particularly in light of findings from two pivotal studies. The research conducted by Doan et al. in 2018 and McGuinness et al. in 2021 highlights a troubling trend: the rate of return on educational investments has been on the decline since the period of 2008 to 2010. This downturn suggests a growing discrepancy between the output of the education system and the demands of the labor market, and the current issues of insufficient returns for rural students.

CHALLENGES:

A misguided dream

In the distinctive context of Vietnam's rural, agriculture-heavy landscapes, the true test of education lies in its ability to deliver visible, tangible benefits to students—manifested through money in hand, real-world experiences, and viable career paths. The luxury of education as an end in itself, a notion often entertained in urban, elite circles as learning for the sake of learning, is a concept rural Vietnam simply cannot afford as it risks undermining all decades-long progress made in boosting enrollment and advocating for education, as students will inevitably disengage if they perceive their learning does not translate into these tangible means. Particularly in these rural settings, the opportunity cost of education soars, given the predominance of low-skilled labor opportunities nearby. Education must therefore ideally offer concrete, practical benefits, ensuring rural students are not merely schooled but truly educated for their own success of social mobility, and further, the development of non-urban communities altogether.

Firstly, this is due to the lack of tailored career pathways and alternatives for rural students. The prevailing educational strategies devised by policymakers and non-profit organizations for rural students are founded on a fundamentally flawed premise of university: that all students, by virtue of completing their education, will be (only) equipped to pursue education training, secure gainful employment, and thereby ascend the social ladder. This assumption is rooted in an urban-centric worldview where the ultimate measure of success is admission to prestigious urban colleges, leading to well-paying jobs and a transformative life trajectory. However, this approach is deeply problematic—it overlooks the needs of those students who, for various reasons, and mostly of it structurally, will be unable to gain university admission. These students, upon completing high school, find themselves without support or direction because the education they received did not prepare them for anything but college.

The idea that university education is the singular goal for all. It's imperative now, more than ever, to pivot away from this narrow conception of university “overemphasis", to protect rural education simply because of how unrealistic, exclusive, and ineffective that goal is to the majority.

This is my reasoning so as to why such final end goal for these students to get into universities are misguided, ineffective and realistic are so forth:

Firstly, the quality of education in rural areas starkly contrasts with that in urban centers. Rural schools lag in teacher expertise, infrastructure, and peer support networks, all of which are critical for success in National Standardized Exams that dictate university admissions. The intensity of academic pressures is also markedly different; rural families often prioritize agricultural work over educational attainment, which fundamentally impacts students' academic engagement.

Secondly, imagine the scenario of thousands of rural students, already structurally disadvantaged with lower quality of education, fewer qualified teachers, and limited access to educational resources, now competing for university slots against their urban counterparts. Given the increasing reliance on elitist admissions criteria, such as IELTS scores, the stark reality is that the odds are heavily stacked against rural students. The competitive disadvantage is further exacerbated by the growing disparities in admission processes, making their prospects of entering prestigious universities increasingly improbable.

Moreover, the structural differences extend way beyond education quality. Household economics play a significant role in defining educational opportunities. The economic landscape for rural families often encompasses immediate monetary needs, job lresponsibilities, and familial obligations, limiting students' ability to engage fully with their educational pursuits. Ethnic minority children, for instance, are more likely to drop out due to the necessity of contributing to agricultural work, a factor compounded by the lower quality of available education as cited by the World Bank in 2019.

Geographical challenges also play a crucial role. Rural students are geographically isolated, which not only restricts their access to quality educational resources but also exists within a community context that may not fully support or value academic achievement. This isolation, combined with lower internet penetration and a community dynamic that often prioritizes immediate economic contributions over educational success, further diminishes their competitive standing in the race for university admissions. These factors collectively highlight the profound systemic issues within the current educational framework. A pivot towards more inclusive and realistic educational goals is essential to protect and enhance rural education, ensuring it is both accessible and relevant to all Vietnamese students, regardless of their geographic or economic background.

And even if rural students can pass these testing points and are thus now qualified enough to access university education and the subsequent career options, this is definitely the small minority of students as the likelihood of achieving such educational and professional heights is markedly slim. Consequently, framing university attendance as the normative goal within teaching practices is not only misguided but also detrimental for all the students who did not have enough luck to achieve that end goal. A shift in focus is needed—one that emphasizes rural-centric educational approaches such as enhanced vocational training, social enterprise education, and specialized agricultural instruction. These alternatives are better aligned with local contexts and can significantly contribute to the development of rural regions by providing practical career paths that are achievable and relevant.

Inadequacy of vocational training and employment-oriented education

The second way, the system fails to improve the future prospects for the majority of their students because of the lack of alternatives and safety nets, other than this very unlikely pathways of continuing their university education.

I argue that the subparness of vocational training education is due to two-fold: (1) Ingrained norms towards the importance of university that leads to the lack of popularity of these program (2) Lack of investment, resources and outdated curriculum that does not serve the students.

Vietnamese culture traditionally places a high value on academic success, often equating it with white-collar jobs seen as more prestigious. This cultural norm tends to overshadow the importance of vocational education, leading to its undervaluation and underinvestment. Rural areas, in particular, suffer from this bias, compounded with the popular narratives coming from government, NGOs glorifying universities, as vocational training is not perceived as a legitimate pathway to success compared to university education. This societal undervaluation creates a lack of interest and motivation among students to pursue vocational tracks, even when they are more practical and aligned with local job market needs. Geographical isolation and limited information dissemination in rural areas also meant that students often have little awareness of potential vocational and technical career paths, especially when it is not being formally pushed as a legitimate option. Without exposure to the range of available options, rural students are likely to overlook vocational training as a viable educational route.

Vocational training centers in rural areas often lack the necessary funding and resources to provide high-quality education. There is a shortage of modern equipment, updated training materials, and qualified instructors who can provide students with the hands-on experience and current industry knowledge they need to be job-ready upon completion of their programs.

Many vocational training programs suffer from outdated curricula that do not match the evolving demands of the job market. The skills taught are often not in sync with the needs of local industries, leading to a mismatch between graduates' skills and available jobs. This misalignment not only hampers students' employment prospects but also fails to support the economic development of the area.

Thirdly, the reasons why I argue that the system fails to serve is because of the inherent failures within the way it operates.

The inadequacy of the current education system in rural areas extends far beyond the mere acquisition of skills and knowledge; it fundamentally fails to foster the intangible qualities that are essential for personal development and social mobility. These qualities, including critical thinking, a mindset geared towards lifelong learning, and the ability to adapt and innovate, are crucial for students to thrive in a rapidly changing world. Unfortunately, for rural students, the education received is often subpar, lacking in both depth and breadth, and fails to instigate the cognitive and attitudinal shifts necessary for meaningful personal and community development. The prevailing educational paradigm falls short of being the revolutionary force necessary to profoundly awaken consciousness or implement the problem-posing education model, as advocated in Paulo Freire's "Pedagogy of the Oppressed." It does not equip students with the critical awareness needed to transcend the confines of their immediate environments—environments often characterized by the reinforcing echo chambers of conventional thought, familial expectations, and societal temptations. True social mobility requires navigating through and beyond these storms of circumstance, a journey that demands a critical and reflective engagement with the world, rather than passive absorption. Yet, this journey becomes formidable within the confines of the "banking model" of education, where knowledge is deposited rather than co-created, leaving little room for the development of the critical consciousness necessary to challenge and change one's socio-economic destiny.

Several factors further impede the quality and tangible benefits students receive from their education:

Lack of quality resources

One of the most critical issues facing rural education is the glaring shortage of skilled educators and quality teaching resources, which are quintessential for delivering a comprehensive education. Rural areas often struggle to attract and retain qualified teachers due to lower salaries, fewer professional development opportunities, and the perceived hardship of working in isolated or less developed areas. Additionally, schools in these regions frequently lack the modern teaching materials and technological tools that can enhance learning experiences and engagement. This resource deficit not only hampers the delivery of curriculum but also significantly affects the ability of students to engage with and absorb complex subjects, ultimately stunting their educational growth and readiness for further education or entry into the workforce.

Problematic ministry culture:

The prevailing ministry culture, focused on superficial metrics such as enrollment rates and standardized test scores ("Chạy theo hình thức"), often diverts attention from more substantive measures of educational quality and the actual impact on an average person’s life. This emphasis on quantitative metrics can lead to policies that prioritize the appearance of success over genuine improvements in educational delivery and outcomes. For instance, schools might be driven to boost graduation rates by any means necessary, which can include lowering standards or neglecting the needs of struggling students, rather than implementing more robust support systems.

This metric-focused approach fails to account for the nuanced realities of rural education, where broad educational engagement and genuine skill acquisition are needed to transform communities. Shifting this culture requires a fundamental change in policy evaluation, moving towards a model that values qualitative assessments of student growth, teacher effectiveness, and community impact. Educational success should be measured not just by the number of students who pass exams, but by how well students are prepared to lead productive and fulfilling lives post-graduation.

—

Despite governmental commitments to enhancing education through competency-based practices and curriculum reforms, a gap remains in the actualization of these intentions. The Ministry of Education and Training (MOET) has identified changes in exam content as a significant hurdle, particularly with the 2015 PISA rankings drop, underscoring a need for curriculum that resonates with modern contexts unfamiliar to Vietnamese students. Associate Professor Tran Kieu highlights a shift towards a learner-centered approach that emphasizes the development of core competencies as crucial for modern education. However, inconsistencies in training quality and implementation capacity persist, partly due to infrastructural and resource limitations. For instance, the introduction of the "whole school" reform showed potential in enhancing cognitive and non-cognitive achievements, yet the variation in student performance underscores disparities in educational engagement across regions.

To bridge these gaps, Vietnam has initiated several teacher training programs and reforms aimed at bolstering competency-based education. Nevertheless, widespread challenges remain in ensuring these programs' effectiveness, largely due to insufficient funding for upgrading educational facilities and a lack of comprehensive guidance on implementing competency-based teaching. The need for more equitable investment in educational infrastructure and materials is paramount for enhancing the overall quality of education across the country. Ultimately, for these reforms to bear fruit, the government must ensure the effective implementation of ongoing policies and programs, such as the Renovation of General Education Project and the Enhancing Teacher Education Program-for-Results. Exploring innovative solutions, such as adaptive learning technologies and high-tech management systems, could further revolutionize the educational landscape, making quality education more accessible and relevant to all students, especially those in rural areas.

Policy recommendations

In Vietnam, a massive increase in intakes of tertiary education was observed in the middle and third parts of 2000s (see Figure 1). The gross enrolment rate of tertiary education stayed stable around 10 per cent for many years prior to 2003 and then increased to 15.9 per cent in 2005. The enrolment rate again increased from 15.9 per cent in 2005 to 24.4 per cent in 2011. Between 2003 and 2011, the gross tertiary enrolment rose by 144 per cent. The increase in the uptake beginning in 2004 would start having an effect in 2008 onwards as the additional students graduated after three or four years of training. The effect in the following years would be stronger because of the incremental number of the graduates. Pressure of oversupply of higher education graduates has led to lower wage rate or higher unemployment rate amongst the graduates (Chevalier and Lindley, 2009; Pecoraro, 2014). The declining returns to education would thus discourage education investment and then harm human capital formation, leading to slowdown of the sustainable economic growth (Booth, 2003). This is a real big risk for sustainable economic growth as the economic growth in the long run relies on productivity (Krugman, 1992)

In addition, quality of the training in Vietnam has not always been followed with the same pace of enrolment scale expansion (Christian, 2014) and the economic growth has been sluggish since the GFC. Therefore, Vietnam’s economy has not been able to take in fully the extra graduates. There is evidence that workers with university degrees have the second highest unemployment rate just after the non-educated group in recent years.3 This is unusual and may suggest an oversupply of the higher education graduates. In addition, the qualifications of higher educated workers are not as high level of quality as they should be. Christian (2014) indicated the unbalanced training in Vietnam education, which has focused mainly on scale expansion, but not the quality of the education per se. The need to focus on both scales including the expansion and the quality of education is raised. It is suggested that it is not just expanding scale attainment, but it is important to provide the right skills to meet Vietnam’s continued economic modernization in the coming decades. Apart from job-specific skills, Vietnamese employers value cognitive skills, such as problem solving and critical thinking, and behavioural skills, such as teamwork and communication. The current tertiary training in Vietnam lacks these skills.

Enhancing vocational education

De-stigmatization of vocational schools: Actively work to remove the stigma associated with vocational education. Promote vocational schools as viable and prestigious alternatives to university education, emphasizing their role in successfully training skilled workers across various sectors.

Curriculum relevance: Ensure that vocational school curricula are closely aligned with real-world demands and local economic needs. The curriculum should be dynamic, student-oriented, and directly linked to actual career opportunities, providing practical skills that meet the current market demands.

Increased investment in vocational education: Redirect a significant portion of the current educational budget towards vocational schools. Focus on enhancing the quality of facilities, equipment, and teaching resources to provide high-value education that offers tangible returns on investment for rural students.

Diversifying educational pathways

Establish clear and respected alternative educational pathways that provide robust training and education. These pathways should offer certifications and qualifications that are recognized by industry and provide real economic opportunities.

Strengthen ties between educational institutions and local industries to facilitate practical training, apprenticeships, and job placement, ensuring the students have secure pathways into the workforce.

Develop support systems that assist students who opt for non-traditional paths or who enter the workforce earlier. This includes career counseling, job placement services, and continuous education opportunities to help them advance in their careers without a traditional university degree.

The plan should also outline the key enabling reforms that are needed to establish appropriate conditions for the effective operation of the existing HEIs from the viewpoint of favorable governance and sustainable financing. The strategic vision accompanied by a fully costed implementation road map should address the following questions: y How will higher education support Vietnam’s socio economic development? y What quantitative targets will the Government pursue with regard to overall enrolment growth, providing equal opportunities of access and success for students from underrepresented groups, raising educational attainment among the adult population, and strengthening the research output and technology transfer results? y What level of public and private resource mobilization would be necessary to achieve the Government’s objectives in a sustainable manner? y Is the current balance between universities, teacher training colleges, and TVET institutions appropriate, considering Vietnam’s human capital requirements and long-term development objectives? Does the system allow students to move easily between subsectors and institutions? y Would Vietnam benefit from establishing a small number of high-quality universities designed to conduct leading-edge research in priority areas? y Are the higher education system’s current QA mechanisms adequate? If not, how can they be improved? y Do the system’s governance arrangements create an appropriate regulatory framework to support innovation and the adoption of international best practices?
