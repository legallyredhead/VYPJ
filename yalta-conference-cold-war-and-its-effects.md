Brief: Yalta Conference, Cold War, and Its Effects

The Yalta conference was the second of the 3 major conferences in the World War II period. It was prompted by the success of the Battle of Normandy, which made the victory of the Allied seem more imminent than ever. Thus, the leaders of the Allied nations - Winston Churchill (Prime Minister of the UK), Franklin D. Roosevelt (President of the United States of America), and Joseph Stalin (Soviet Premier) - met in Yalta to discuss the next courses of action after a defeat of Germany and the Axis Powers.

The conference produced the Yalta Protocol, a top-secret document containing 03 areas: division of lands, reparations, and a proposal for a world organization.

The Yalta Protocol’s first section specified the establishment of a conference to discuss the proposed “World Organization”, with the participants being existing United Nations countries as of 8th February 1945 and the nations that had declared war on the common enemy by 1st March 1945 (which are meant to be the eight Associated Nations and Turkey), as well as the Soviet Socialist Republics. This section, created to unite nations against the Axis Powers and establish international peace and consensus after WWII, paved the way for the official establishment of the United Nations in the future and its procedures as well as the confirmation of Stalin for the Soviets to take part in the organization, which was previously only an agreement between Churchill and Roosevelt in 1941 (History Channel, 2009).

The conference also discussed the reshaping of Germany and Europe following the end of WWII. Several reparation terms were drafted, and Germany was asked to pay all of the losses as well as lose most of its military capacity. The most important outcome, however, was the division of land and “spheres of influence”. First of all, Germany would be divided among the 4 countries: the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and France. Berlin, the capital of Germany, lay in the Soviet-occupied zone, but was also divided into 4, each piece taken by an Allied country. This division is thought to be one of the causes of the Cold War, in which Berlin became a heated theatre.

In persuasion of the Soviet’s participation in the Indochina region against Japan, where the other Allied nations were struggling to keep up, Stalin was granted a “sphere of influence” in Eastern Europe (Office of the Historian, n.d.). This grant of influence allowed the Soviets to expand their political agenda and acquire more land and satellite states, which followed their Communist ideology, causing future divisions among the Allied nations as the United States and the USSR faced each other in a battle of dominance.

Meanwhile, with the help of Stalin, Japan's defeat in the Asia-Pacific theatre was achieved. This ending, however, was far from peace for the whole world, as decades of the Cold War were about to emerge. Many countries were forced to split into two, usually a communist-rooted and a democratic-rooted government - due to internal conflict or international influences. Among them were China, Korea, and Vietnam.

Soon, tension arose between the two main ideologies that were dominating the world: Communism/Socialism and Democracy. During this time, the U.S. and the U.S.S.R. continuously engaged in battles of technology, development, and proxy wars, both desperate to expand their influence. Most prominent were the threats of nuclear bombing, which required intervention from the UNSC and other international bodies to cease from happening. As for the aforementioned countries that were split, some came out united, such as Vietnam, which became a Socialist nation, while others were not so lucky as the two halves remain split and hostile to this day, such as North and South Korea. In 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed, concluding the Cold War period, and so, a century of diplomatic tasks to normalize relations commenced.

Nowadays, the world is a very different place. Even though many countries stuck with their ideology even after the collapse of the Soviet Union, there have been many developments and renovations to better themselves and encourage globalization. Vietnam is an interesting case to look at, as it has been most persistent in its ideology, while also changing itself with time and achieving many amazing accomplishments on the world stage. The country has undergone an economic reform, also known as the “doi moi” period, where the free market was legalized after assistance from the Soviet Union dwindled and the country was met with an economic crash. Since then, there have been major improvements in the economic-politics scene in Vietnam, as the country rose from isolation to prosperity. It became one of the world’s largest rice exporters and is on the rise of becoming a key player in many industries. Politically, the country also reconciled and established diplomatic relations with many other nations. It is a member of multiple international bodies, while also being the potential ally and strategic partner of world powers such as the United States, Russia, and China. However, it is important to note that these achievements were gathered with Vietnam staying true to its founding principles, even if changes do take place to accommodate advancements.

In conclusion, it is clear that the world has become a more globalized and united stage, as past conflicts are resolved and diplomatic relations prosper. Though the effects of the Yalta Conference leading up to the Cold War will be an unerasable mark in history, it has also served as a lesson for world leaders to learn from. As countries like Vietnam continues to evolve, the world is likely to become a less polarized place, where unity and collaboration thrives.
