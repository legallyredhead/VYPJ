Why Do Laws Protecting Vietnamese Children Fail to Empower Their Decision-Making?

Abstract

This journal offers an examination of the effectiveness of existing legal framework on children’s participation rights in Vietnam and the challenges of implementing the Convention of the Rights of the Child in the country, including traditional customs and culture, and socioeconomic situations.

Introduction

Children’s participation rights has been increasingly discussed since the 1990s with the existence of the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) (Wyness, 2013). Vietnam is the first country in Asia and the second country globally to become a member of the CRC, and has since demonstrated its efforts and commitment to integrating the principles of the CRC into its legal system, such as amending the Law on Protection, Care, and Education of Children to the Child Law in 2016, revising key provisions in the Criminal Law, Civil Law, Labor Law, and Education Law towards a child rights-based approach. However, admist these remarkable development, the real life implementation of children's participation rights still predominantly remains formalistic, superficial, and lacks adequate attention from adult stakeholders. Periodic assessment by the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child reveals persistent challenges in upholding Article 12 of the CRC—"respect for the views of the child"— across nations, specifically from significant cultural norms and societal biases. This journal will comprehensively evaluate current Vietnamese law on children's participation rights and analyze practical challenges in applying the CRC Convention in Vietnam.

Policy Overview: CRC

The purpose of the Convention of the Rights of the Child (CRC) is to shift the perspective of considering children from objects of rights to subjects of rights, viewing children as mere recipients of rights to recognizing them as active participants entitled to political, civil, economic, cultural, and social rights, akin to any other member of society. Most importantly, these rights must warrant equal respect, implementation and protection. The Convention becomes an international legal framework for member countries to build and perfect their national legal systems on children's rights. Member States should ensure that their national laws are compatible with the principles in the Convention; Regulations, programs and policies need to be based on children's rights and ensure the core principles of the Convention. This article will use the CRC Convention as a legal framework to analyze the current legal system on children's participation rights in Vietnam.

Policy Analysis: Vietnam’s legal framework

Definition of the child

According to Vietnam's Children's Law in 2016, children are people under 16 years old, which is 2 years younger than general international regulations. Vietnam is in the 11 countries that set the age of children lower than the CRC Convention, including: North Korea (17), Timor Leste (17), Myanmar, Cambodia (16), Cuba (16), Kyrgyzstan (16). 16), Indonesia (15), Saudi Arabia (15) and Iran (shockingly, being 9).

The last paragraph of Article 1 of the CRC Convention states “Except in the case the law [of a Member State] is applicable to the child of an earlier age of maturity” to make this provision more specific, but this does not mean that flexibility should be allowed in determining the range of childhod. This provision means that national members can determine the age of maturity earlier, but are till obliged to monitor CRC provisions for everyone under 18 years of age. Countries can only set a minimum age lower than 18 in some areas and for some specific purposes - provided that the general principles of the CRC are met.

On the other hand, it is especially worth noting that the the definition of the child in Vietnam’s legal documents are inconsistent. In Vietnam’s 2015 Civil Code, the age of maturity is 18 years old, while the 2019 Labor Code stipulates that minor workers are workers under 18 years old and the concept of children is understood as people under 15 years old (Article 143). Moreover, the Law on Handling of Administrative Violations 2012 stated that people aged 14 years or older can be punished for administrative violations (Point a, Clause 1, Article 5). Thus, the concept of a child is not identical to the concept of a minor (a person under 18 years old), although both of these concepts refer to a person who has not yet fully developed physically, mentally and do not near the legal rights and obligations of adults. Subjects aged 16 to under 18 years old are left open, with no clear regulations.

This shows how Vietnam has failed to establish a consistent legal age framework that aligns with the principles of the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC).

Legal recognition of children’s rights

The Constitution of Vietnam, the highest legal document in the system of legal regulations, acknowledges children's participation rights: "Children shall be protected, cared for, educated by the State, families, and society; they shall participate in issues concerning children..." (Article 37). The 2013 Constitution serves as a guideline for the State to formulate and enact other laws, including the 2016 Law on Children. While the title might suggest that its provisions primarily concern children as the subjects of rights, Article 3 of the 2016 Law on Children expands its scopes as follows: “State agencies, political organizations, socio-political organizations, socio-political-professional organizations, social organizations, socio-professional organizations, economic organizations, public service units, armed forces units, educational institutions, families, Vietnamese citizens; international organizations, foreign organizations operating within the territory of Vietnam, and individuals who are foreign residents in Vietnam.”

A set of laws specifically dedicated to children, outlining their rights, yet children are not the direct recipients of the law. Additionally, there’s a section allocated solely to emphasize the duties that children must fulfill, including the duties of children towards their families, the community, their homeland, and themselves (Articles 37 to 40). This reflects the perspective that children are still regarded primarily as objects in need of adult care and protection, rather than being approached based on their rights.

It is evident that these provisions may not hold legal weight because if children fail to fulfill these duties, the responsibility falls on adults. For instance, duties for children such as "No gambling; no buying, selling, or using addictive substances” cannot be carried out by children themselves without guidance from adults (family, school, and the state). Therefore, instead of framing these as children's duties, it would be more legally sound to approach them from the perspective of children's rights to be protected from these harmful factors, and the responsibility of adults, rather than the current view of the child.

Lack of Clarity and Specificity

Upon initial inspection, Vietnam's framework for children's participation rights appears comprehensive, clearly outlined across numerous provisions. However, upon closer examination, many still lack precision and transparency, especially concerning their legal implications.

For instance, Article 34 of the Vietnam’s 2016 Law on Children outlines the following: "Children have the right to express their opinions, desires regarding issues related to children; they are free to assemble according to the provisions of the law appropriate to their age, maturity level, and development; they have the right for authorities, organizations, educational institutions, families, and individuals to listen, understand, and respond to their legitimate opinions and desires." Compared to Article 12 of the CRC: “(1) States Parties shall assure to the child who is capable of forming his or her own views the right to express those views freely in all matters affecting the child, the views of the child being given due weight in accordance with the age and maturity of the child.”

While Article 12 of the Convention uses the phrase "given due weight," which imply "to be given appropriate consideration," Article 34 uses the phrase "chính đáng", According to the Hoàng Phê Vietnamese Dictionary, "chính đáng" means correct, justifiable. "Justifiable" implies compliance with requirements and meeting needs. This phrase carries a lower legal significance, hiding the role of adults in meeting the needs and desires of children. This fails to emphasize the importance of valuing children's viewpoints, rather than requiring them to justify their needs in order to be heard.

Moreover, the 2016 Law on Children, along with Decree 56/2017/ND-CP, provides detailed provisions regarding the responsibilities of entities (agencies, organizations, educational institutions, families, and individuals) in ensuring the implementation of children's participation rights. However, this does not offer specific guidance and explanations for cases where children's participation rights are not properly exercised "for the best interests of the child." This absence on "what constitutes the best interests of the child" or "alternative measures" could easily lead to abuse by authorities. In General Comment No. 14 (2013), the Committee emphasized the most effective means of determining ‘the best interests of the child’ is by actively listening to the child's perspective."

Impact on Youth

The ambiguous and inconsistent legal framework regarding children's participation rights in Vietnam can have significant implications for its youth population. In the immediate sense, the lack of clarity and specificity in legal provisions undermines the ability of Vietnamese youth to effectively exercise their rights to participate in decisions that affect us.

Article 34 of Vietnam’s 2016 Law on Children grants children the right to express their opinions and desires, yet the vague language surrounding how these opinions should be considered and acted upon leaves room for interpretation and inconsistent application. As a result, we find their voices muted or disregarded in matters that directly impact their lives, which is entirely true in many Vietnamese families. These issues can even reach beyond individual experiences to affect the broader Vietnamese society, where a generation of youth who are inadequately prepared to participate in governance and community affairs may contribute to social challenges like social inequality, lack of transparency in governance, and reduced accountability, etc.

Personal Commentary & Recommendation

I believe that there are several factors contributing to the challenges regarding the implementation of children’s participation rights in Vietnam from CRC’s guidelines: traditional customs and culture, and socioeconomic conditions. These are surely “big words” - overarching concepts that are not easily changed, but there are still steps we can take to eliminate barriers to children’s participation.

Traditional customs and Culture

The right to participate can be understood, constructed, and implemented differently across various cultural backgrounds and social contexts. Even adults' perceptions of children's participation rights are influenced by the cultural and social norms of that society.

In Vietnam, child participation is not prevalent, and the youngest members of the society rarely get the chance to engage in family discussions and decision-making processes. The notion of "filial piety" (Hiếu) from Confucianism traditions is a significant factor in defining one's role as a child. It often idealizes parental authority and emphasizes children's obligations to a large extend. Although nowadays, imposed ideologies on children in Vietnam are gradually being eradicated, in many families, especially in rural areas, children are still often pressured by their parents on matters ranging from education and career choices to marriage and having children.

The emphasis on "filial piety" and the obligation to "obey" not only cause parents or other family members to forget their own duties and fail to listen to the opinions of children but also render children passive, only following their parents' opinions or not daring to voice their own opinions. This can be considered one of the biggest barriers to implementing participation rights in Vietnam because children's participation practices start with the smallest things right within the family.

Changing perceptions and viewpoints require effort from all stakeholders, not only the community and the state but also from schools, families, and children themselves. As mentioned above, The legal framework in Vietnam on children’s participation rights has some provisions that are not mutually compatible and with the principles and provision set out in the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and some provisions lacks legal validity - these need to be amended. It is also essential to complete the guidance documents for relevant stakeholders to implement and ensure participation rights of children.

Economic and social situation

The economic and social situation deeply affects ensuring children's participation rights. As of 2020, Vietnam still had about four million children lacking access to at least two basic social services, including education, healthcare, nutrition, housing, clean water and sanitation, information, social integration, and protection. In some underdeveloped areas, many children are even forced out of their childhood life to become the main breadwinners to support their families. According to the International Labour Organization (ILO). "58.8% of working children in Vietnam are child laborers.” Children's participation in labor restrict their time and their rights to participate in other social activities.

Additionally, there’s a limited understanding of the laws regarding children's participation rights in lower-income society. Children require the support and guidance of adults to effectively exercise their participation rights. Adults, especially parents or guardians, must also understand children's participation rights for children to participate effectively. Children need to be guided and educated about their participation rights and how to exercise them. However, many parents, especially in lower-income community, often lack knowledge about children's rights. They may believe that providing material necessities is sufficient, overlooking the importance of respecting children's opinions and involving them in decision-making processes.

Clearly, the state requires economic resources in terms of infrastructure, transportation, technological equipment, especially in the era of the 4.0 technological revolution, to adapt to new changes and global developments in ensuring human rights as well as children's participation rights. This includes providing equipment and information technology applications to serve everyone, including children; digitizing information, using connectivity applications to help children access information and policies easily; expressing their opinions, recommendations; or reporting, lodging complaints about rights violations.

Conclusion

Despite Vietnam's commitment to children's rights, cultural norms and vague laws keep the youngest voices unheard. Vietnam has made commendable strides in ensuring children’s participation rights in the legal system. The ratification of the CRC and incorporation of children’s participation rights into the Vietnam Constitution and the Children Law 2016 have demonstrated the Vietnamese government’s commitment to promoting and protecting children’s rights. Nevertheless, several challenges persist in ensuring meaningful and effective participation of children in Vietnam. The government needs to consider factors including politics, economic, and culture to create a human right based approach to reaching children rights in society.
