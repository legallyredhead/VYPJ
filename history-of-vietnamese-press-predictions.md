Op-Ed: History of Vietnamese Press & Predictions

The Vietnamese Press history dates back to 1865, when Gia Dinh Bao became the first Vietnamese newspaper permitted to publish, effectively introducing a new meaning to information communication and the Vietnamese language to the country. Since then, multiple forms of media have developed in Vietnam, playing an important role in the country’s communication and diplomacy during both war and peace times.

During the colonial and wartime periods, the press was widely used as an information-spreading tool to promote revolutionary ideologies. In 1925, the Youth Newspaper was established, marking the beginning of the revolutionary press in Vietnam. It is worth noting that these newspaper outlets were met with extreme censorship. Le Paria (Người cùng khổ) - a newsletter founded by Ho Chi Minh, who later became the leader of the Vietnamese Communist Revolution - was heavily scrutinized and threatened by the French authorities for its political content (Lao Dong, 2024). However, with persistent efforts, Vietnamese Communist newspapers prospered, and the following decades witnessed the founding of many other Communist publishing firms. Journalists serving in these newspaper firms were also considered revolutionaries, whose weapon was the power of their pen and words.

By the time the Vietnamese Communist government was established, the press industry had taken a clear shape. This model of the Press was similar to that of the now-dissolved Soviet Union, in which publishing firms operated strictly under the supervision of the government, solely for propaganda and governmental issuing of information purposes. Unlike Western media, which was reader-centric and included advertisements, the Vietnamese press, like that of the Soviet Union, was regulated by the Propaganda and Training Committee and free of the pressure to make a profit (Dang, 2012), thus, most of the information issued was not focused on the needs of the news consumers.

It was not until the “doi moi” era that this changed. With the renovation and development of the economy, the press became more conscious of the economic size of newspaper publishing. With the permission to include advertisements in these news channels by 1989, royalties for journalists no longer came from just the government, but also from the profits the firm was making from the readers and the private companies who requested advertisements in the newspaper. With this change in policy, the Vietnamese press became a competitive industry, catering more to the reader’s needs. In fact, then, the criticism of governmental officials and other disruptive activities was promoted by the press. The General Secretary of the Vietnamese Communist Party at the time, Mr. Nguyen Van Linh, encouraged journalists to report true and accurate information (Le, 2013). In his 400-word letter named “Things That Must Be Done Immediately” (“Những việc cần làm ngay”) published in the Nhan Dan newspaper, Mr. Linh emphasized the need for Vietnamese citizens to point out and criticize the wrongdoings of the government in the new era (Nhan Dan, n.d.), thus, combating negativity and steer towards a happier future for Vietnam.

However, this trend in the press was met with a paradox, as the government continued to be the main regulator of the Vietnamese press. Specifically, after the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, the Vietnamese government became hyperaware of the possibility of a revolution stemming from a loose regulation of the press, thus, policies regarding criticism of the government in the press were tightened.

Despite that, the press continues to advance alongside the economy. In the Vietnamese Press Law, it is stated that journalists have the right to collect and report information, and any actions prohibiting such tasks are illegal. The press also became bolder with their reports in recent years, as many scandals of high-ranking officials make the front page of newsletters. Despite this development, the Vietnamese press continues to be state-owned, with regulations covering the information allowed to be released, which has to align with the Socialist Republic of Vietnam’s ideologies and diplomatic positions. The Vietnamese press will likely remain state-owned in the future, however, the current reports suggest that this industry will be much less financially dependent on the government in the following decades, as several cuts in the press’ budget are to be announced by the government. This means that journalism will become more reader-oriented and rely on advertisements more to fund its publishing process. Competition in the press industry, in addition, will also rise as firms compete for their audience’s attention and choice.

In the modern world, the role of journalism is changing rapidly. What started solely as a means for the Vietnamese government to introduce information and propaganda to the general public has evolved into something more suitable for the current economic-political climate. Its purpose is now to cater for both the Communist Party and the readers, providing information on a multifaceted level, allowing its audience to catch up with the socio-political development of the world, as well as their enjoyment and entertainment. Therefore, it would be wrong to assume that the Vietnamese press nowadays is a heavily censored industry, as viewed by many Western press industries, but rather a balanced mix of servitude towards the Communist Party’s political agenda and the audience’s demand. Moreover, it is imminent that this industry will continue to be tweaked and modified in the upcoming decades, steering toward a more reader-centric approach, whilst continuing to combat and prevent the spread of false or politically sensitive information.

In conclusion, journalism in Vietnam is not only the voice of the government, but also that of the citizens, a mixture that works well amidst the globalization era for a developing and ever-changing country like Vietnam. Throughout the past, the industry has gone through various stages of development, from acting as a political weapon in wartimes (e.g. Voice of Vietnam radio or Youth newspaper) to being a medium for the spreading of information and the voice of citizens. This is a commendable journey, and alongside the development of the economic and political playground, as well as to accommodate new press policies, the press of Vietnam will continue to develop to fulfill its role and ability as a companion to the government and the citizens.
