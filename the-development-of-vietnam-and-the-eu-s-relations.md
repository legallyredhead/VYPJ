The Development of Vietnam and the EU's Relations

Vietnam has been an extremely amicable player in the diplomatic stage ever since its Congress passed the “Doi Moi” bill allowing for integration in 1986, and its established diplomatic as well as economic ties with the EU is an important milestone in Vietnam’s new quest for international cooperation, as it is one of the events that signify the end of the post-Cold War isolation and polarisation era for the Southeast Asian country.

In 1990, Vietnam and the EU shook hands for the first time and established diplomatic ties, ending years of Western sanctions and hostile relations. However, cooperation during this beginning era proved to be limited, as the focus was mainly placed on the return of displaced Vietnamese people and pushing post-war reparations rather than other advancement causes (Tran, n.d.). The relationship took a big leap in 1995 after the signing of the Framework Cooperation Agreement (FCA) between the two parties. In this document, Vietnam’s efforts in international integration were recognized, and the treaty facilitated the establishment of trade and cooperation in several fields such as technology and communication with the foundation being respect for human rights principles and support for Vietnam’s transition into the “Doi Moi” era. With this agreement in force, Vietnam and the EU established a bond that would only be strengthened with time. Then, under the treaty, the EU made strives to help Vietnam emerge in the global economy through multiple programs, namely the 1994-1999 technology and economic-centric program EuroTAPViet and the 1998-2017 market-centric one called MUTRAP (Vu, n.d.).

Towards the end of the 1990s to the 2000s, the Vietnam-EU relationship grew in prominence through the year. In these years, the EU continued to aid Vietnam in efforts to mitigate poverty and further improve its market strategies and mechanisms, promoting sustainable financial practices. This assistance has been fundamental in maintaining Vietnam’s position on the global stage, allowing Vietnam to align with international economic goals. Moreover, recognizing the growing importance of the relationship as well as the ever-changing trend of the world, Vietnam and the EU started working on a new relationship framework - a document that would be more inclusive and offer a wider range of area coverage suitable for the time period.

The 2010s marked the most important milestones in the development of the Vietnam-EU relationship with the signing of two fundamental agreements: the Partnership and Cooperation Agreement (PCA), for which the negotiation process had begun in the last decade, and the EU-Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA). While the previous agreement (the FCA) only covered basic points relating to trade and human rights, the new deal (the PCA, signed in 2012 and came into force in 2016) expanded the scope of cooperation to virtually every era, including environment, science, tourism, culture, and crime resistance. Under this new cooperation treaty, Vietnam and the EU are able to engage in more intensive discussions, and thus, several recurrent meetings such as the annual EU-Vietnam enhanced Human Rights Dialogue and the EU-Vietnam Political Consultations were founded. Moreover, Vietnam and the EU also established grounds for defense cooperation through the Framework Participation Agreement (FDA) in late 2019, in which the two parties agreed on bilateral aid in defense operations, leading to the involvement of Vietnamese personnel in EU peacekeeping missions (Press and information team of the EEAS, 2023).

In addition, economic ties have always been one of the main points of Vietnam-EU’s cooperation, and there is no doubt that improvements were made in that regard as well. The signing of the EVFTA (signed in 2019, effective since 2020) is responsible for such advancement, as, under this economic agreement, Vietnam became the EU’s leading ASEAN trade partner. In fact, before the EVFTA, trade increase between Vietnam and the EU was below the line of Vietnam’s overall growth. With the effects of the EVFTA, which aims to gradually remove tariffs on almost all Vietnamese products (99.7% of Vietnam’s exports after 7 years to be specific), trades are predicted to rise exponentially between the two parties, allowing Vietnam to compensate for its trade deficit with other nations while pushing the development of Vietnam’s key global value chains (Pham, 2020). Moreover, the EU and Vietnam continue to strengthen such an economic relationship with the enforcement of the EVFTA, resulting in more collaborative programs as well as a better risk management technique that proved to be necessary during the COVID-19 era. As a result, Vietnam’s exports had indeed risen in value just a year after the signing of the agreement; however, there is still room for a more proper implementation of the deal. For example, Vietnam should catch more interest for investment from other EU countries outside of the few central ones that are currently investing or there should be more programs to aid Vietnam’s economic transition to suit the 4.0 technological era, with the focus being placed on sustainable development, digital conversion, and human resources (Vo, 2022).

On the bright side, the two parties are both looking forward to the advancement of the bilateral cooperation. On May 9, 2024, the delegation of the EU paid Vietnam a visit, and during this meeting, the two parties both recognizes the value of their collaboration. Vietnam hopes that in the near future, the EVFTA will be further implemented as well as the ratification of the EU – Vietnam Investment Protection Agreement (EVIPA) - an agreement that aims to overcome the challenges left by the EVFTA and thus ensure economic collaboration between the two parties - will be successful. Moreover, in the same year, the EU has also voiced their interest in upgrading the Vietnam-EU ties further, offering praises to the Vietnamese government as well as potential assistance in areas such as maritime protection and internet security.

In conclusion, it seems like the Vietnam-EU relationship will continue to grow in the future as both parties are each other’s important economic partners and supporters in the world stage. It is predicted that in the next decades, Vietnam and the EU will continue to foster a strong relationship, including a possible upgrade in diplomatic title, progress in the ratification and implementation of the current agreements, and the addition of programs and assistance for a wide variety of areas. The partnership between Vietnam and the EU is indeed a promising and mutually beneficial one, thus, advancement is imminent for the two parties. Perhaps an even more culturally integrated future is ahead of them.
